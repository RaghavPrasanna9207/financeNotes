## 1. What the balance sheet shows

The **balance sheet** is a **snapshot** of what a company **owns** (assets) and **owes** (liabilities) on **one specific date**, usually 31 March (the financial year-end). Listed companies also publish one at the half-year (30 September).

If the P&L is a video of the year, the balance sheet is a **photograph** at the end of it.

### The accounting equation
```
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity
```
Or put another way: **everything the company owns was financed either by borrowing (liabilities) or by owners (equity).**

The two sides **always balance**, which is where the name comes from.

Indian balance sheets (Schedule III format) list **Equity and Liabilities** first and **Assets** second.

## 3. The liabilities side explained

### 3.1 Shareholders' equity (net worth / book value)
```
Shareholders' equity = Share capital + Reserves & surplus
```
- **Share capital:** number of shares × **face value** (₹1, ₹2, ₹5 or ₹10). This is a legal/accounting number, **not** the market price or the amount investors actually paid.
  - If shares were issued above face value, the excess goes to **securities premium** (part of reserves).
- **Reserves & surplus (other equity):** mainly **retained earnings**, the accumulated profits not paid out as dividends. Each year: `Closing reserves ≈ Opening reserves + PAT − Dividends`.
- **Net worth = what shareholders "own" on paper:** what would theoretically be left if all assets were sold at book value and all liabilities paid.
- **Book value per share** = Net worth / shares = 6,750 / 100 = **₹67.50**. (The market price of ₹540 is 8× this, because the market values ABC's future earnings and brand, which aren't on the balance sheet.)

**What to look for:**
- **Steadily rising reserves** → the company is profitable and retaining earnings. Good.
- **Share capital rising** without bonus issues or splits → **new shares are being issued** (dilution). Check why (Chapter 4.7).
- **Negative net worth** → accumulated losses exceed capital. A serious red flag.

### 3.2 Borrowings (debt)
- **Long-term borrowings:** loans, bonds or debentures repayable after more than 12 months.
- **Short-term borrowings:** working capital loans, commercial paper, repayable within 12 months.
- **Current maturities of long-term debt:** the portion of long-term debt due in the next 12 months.
- **Lease liabilities** (under Ind AS 116): obligations under long-term leases (shops, offices). Debt-like.

**What to look for:**
- **Debt-to-equity ratio** (Chapter 4.5): ABC = 500 / 6,750 = **0.07**, practically debt-free.
- **Trend:** rising debt with flat profits is dangerous.
- **What it's used for:** debt for productive capacity (a new plant) is very different from debt to pay salaries or dividends.

### 3.3 Trade payables
- Money the company **owes its suppliers** for goods and services already received.
- It's effectively an **interest-free loan from suppliers**. Strong companies with bargaining power (like Asian Paints or D-Mart) can pay suppliers later.
- **Payable days** = Trade payables / COGS × 365 = 1,500 / 5,500 × 365 ≈ **100 days**.

### 3.4 Other liabilities and provisions
- **Advances from customers** (money received before delivery, which is common in real estate and capital goods)
- **Provisions:** estimated future obligations (gratuity, warranties, legal claims)
- **Deferred tax liability:** tax that will be payable in future because of timing differences
- **Statutory dues:** GST, TDS payable

### 3.5 Contingent liabilities (in the notes, not on the balance sheet)
- **Possible** obligations that depend on future events: pending lawsuits, tax disputes, guarantees given for others' loans.
- They aren't on the balance sheet but are disclosed in the **notes to accounts**.
- **Red flag** if they're large relative to net worth (e.g. more than 20–30%), especially guarantees to promoter group companies.

## 4. The assets side explained

### 4.1 Property, plant & equipment (PPE / fixed assets)
- Land, buildings, plant, machinery, vehicles, furniture: long-term assets used to run the business.
- Shown at **net block** = gross block (original cost) − accumulated depreciation.
- **Asset-heavy businesses** (steel, cement, telecom, power) have large PPE. **Asset-light businesses** (software, brands, platforms) have small PPE, which often means higher returns on capital.

### 4.2 Capital work in progress (CWIP)
- Assets **under construction**: a new plant being built, not yet operational.
- Once complete, CWIP moves into PPE and starts depreciating.
- **Large CWIP** = expansion underway → **future** revenue growth (if the project succeeds).
- **Red flag:** CWIP that stays large for many years (a project stuck, or a way to park money).

### 4.3 Intangible assets and goodwill
- **Intangibles:** software, licences, patents, trademarks, acquired brands.
- **Goodwill:** arises when a company **acquires** another for more than the fair value of its net assets. The premium is recorded as goodwill.
- **Watch out:** large goodwill means past acquisitions at high prices. If an acquisition disappoints, goodwill is **impaired** (written down), causing a big loss.

### 4.4 Investments
- **Non-current:** stakes in subsidiaries or associates, long-term bonds and funds.
- **Current:** mutual funds, short-term bonds (treasury surplus).
- Cash-rich companies park surplus here. It's the source of "other income" in the P&L.
- **Red flag:** large investments in or **loans to promoter group companies**.

### 4.5 Inventories
- Raw materials, work-in-progress and finished goods not yet sold.
- **Inventory days** = Inventory / COGS × 365 = 1,800 / 5,500 × 365 ≈ **119 days**.
- **Rising inventory days** may mean products aren't selling, or there's over-production or obsolete stock.
- Varies hugely by industry: FMCG and restaurants are low, jewellery and real estate are very high.

### 4.6 Trade receivables (debtors)
- Money **owed to the company by customers** for goods already sold on credit.
- **Debtor days (receivable days)** = Receivables / Revenue × 365 = 1,200 / 10,000 × 365 ≈ **44 days**.
- **Red flag:** receivables growing **faster than revenue**. The company may be pushing sales with loose credit, booking fictitious sales, or customers may be struggling to pay.
- Watch for large **"provision for doubtful debts"**.

### 4.7 Cash and bank balances
- Liquidity. A healthy cash pile gives safety and flexibility.
- **Net debt** = Borrowings − Cash and liquid investments = 500 − 600 − (liquid investments) → ABC is **net cash**.
- **Red flag:** a large cash balance **and** large borrowings at the same time, with low interest income (where's the cash really?). This was a famous pattern in some frauds.

### 4.8 Other assets
- Loans and advances, prepaid expenses, tax refunds due, capital advances.
- **Watch for:** large "loans and advances" to related parties.

## 5. Working capital

### Definition
```
Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities
```
It measures the money tied up in day-to-day operations: inventory + receivables − payables.

### The cash conversion cycle (CCC)
```
CCC = Inventory days + Receivable days − Payable days
ABC Paints: 119 + 44 − 100 ≈ 63 days
```
- **The time between paying cash for raw materials and collecting cash from customers.**
- **Shorter is better.** Less cash is needed to fund growth.
- Some excellent businesses have **negative** working capital (they collect from customers before paying suppliers): supermarkets, FMCG companies with strong distributor networks, airlines, subscription businesses. Growth actually *generates* cash for them.
- **Rising CCC** means more cash gets stuck in operations as the company grows. That's a drag on free cash flow.

### Why it matters
A company can show strong profit growth while working capital eats all the cash. You'll see this when **CFO lags PAT** (Chapter 4.4).

## 6. Reading a balance sheet over time

Compare **5–10 years** (Screener shows this directly):

| What to compare | Healthy pattern | Warning pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Reserves | Rising steadily | Flat or falling |
| Share capital | Constant (except bonus/split) | Rising (dilution) |
| Borrowings | Low, flat or falling | Rising faster than profits |
| Fixed assets + CWIP | Growing with revenue | Large CWIP stuck for years |
| Receivables & inventory | Growing in line with or slower than revenue | Growing much faster than revenue |
| Cash & investments | Building up | Large cash + large debt simultaneously |

### Linking the balance sheet with the P&L
- **Reserves increase ≈ PAT − dividends.** If reserves grow much less than retained profit, look for write-offs or adjustments that bypassed the P&L.
- **Fixed asset growth** should eventually show up as **revenue growth**. Asset turnover (Revenue / Total assets) tells you how productively assets are used.
- **Debt increase** should show up as **higher interest** in the P&L.

---

## 7. Balance sheet warning signs (summary)
1. **High and rising debt** relative to equity and profits
2. **Receivables and inventory growing faster than sales**
3. **Large CWIP stuck for years**
4. **Large goodwill** from expensive acquisitions
5. **Loans or investments to promoter group / related parties**
6. **Large contingent liabilities** (in the notes)
7. **Share capital rising** (dilution)
8. **Cash and debt both high** with low interest income
9. **Negative net worth**

---

## 8. Summary
- **Assets = Liabilities + Equity.** A snapshot on one date.
- **Equity** = share capital + reserves (retained profits). Rising reserves are good. Rising share capital = dilution.
- **Borrowings:** check D/E and the trend. Lease liabilities are debt-like.
- **Assets:** fixed assets and CWIP (capacity), goodwill (acquisitions), investments, inventory, receivables, cash.
- **Working capital and the cash conversion cycle** (inventory days + debtor days − payable days): shorter is better, negative is excellent.
- Read **5–10 years** and link with the P&L.

## Self-check questions
1. A company has total assets of ₹2,000 Cr and liabilities (excluding equity) of ₹1,200 Cr. What's its net worth?
2. Why is the face value of a share not the same as its market price or book value?
3. Revenue grew 10% but receivables grew 35%. What might this indicate?
4. A company's inventory days are 60, debtor days 30, payable days 75. What's its cash conversion cycle, and is that good?
5. What is CWIP, and why could a large, long-standing CWIP be a concern?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. ₹800 Cr.
2. Face value is a nominal legal amount. Book value = net worth per share. Market price reflects expectations of future earnings.
3. Aggressive credit to push sales, customers struggling to pay, or possibly inflated sales.
4. 60 + 30 − 75 = 15 days. Short, which is good: little cash is tied up.
5. Capital work in progress: assets under construction. If it stays large for years, the project may be stuck, or the money may be parked or misused.
