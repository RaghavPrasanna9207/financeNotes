# Chapter 4.7: Red Flags: How to Spot Trouble Early

> **Core**

> **What you'll learn:** the three biggest red flags (promoter pledging, profit without cash, equity dilution) in depth, plus governance, accounting, balance sheet and business red flags, lessons from real Indian blow-ups, and a red-flag checklist you can run in 15 minutes on Screener.in.

---

## 1. Why red flags matter

Finding a great company is hard. **Avoiding a disaster is easier, and more important.** A stock that falls 90% needs a 900% gain just to break even. Most retail investor wealth destruction in Indian stocks has come from companies that showed **warning signs years in advance**.

**Rule:** one red flag means investigate. Several means **walk away**. There are thousands of other companies.

---

## 2. Red flag #1: Promoter pledging

### What it is
**Promoters** (founders or controlling shareholders) **pledge their shares as collateral** for loans, either personal loans or loans for other group companies.

### Why it's dangerous: the pledge spiral
1. The promoter borrows against shares, say with a loan-to-value of 50%.
2. The **share price falls** (for any reason).
3. The collateral value falls, so the **lender asks for more shares or cash** (a margin call).
4. If the promoter can't provide them, the **lender sells the pledged shares** in the market.
5. Selling pushes the **price down further**, triggering more margin calls and more selling.
6. The promoter may **lose control** of the company, and **minority shareholders suffer huge losses**.

### Signals
- **Pledged %** (Screener: "Pledged percentage", or in the quarterly shareholding pattern): the share of promoter holding that is pledged.
- **Concern:** anything **above ~10–20%**, or **rising over quarters**.
- **Why are they pledging?** It often means the promoter or group is **cash-strapped**, or funding other ventures with this company's shares.

### Examples (historical)
Several Indian groups saw sharp share price collapses linked to high promoter pledging, including companies in the Zee/Essel group (2019), DHFL and others, where pledged share sales accelerated the falls.

## 3. Red flag #2: Profits rising, cash flow not following

### The pattern
- **Reported PAT keeps growing**, but **operating cash flow (CFO) is flat, falling or negative**.
- **Receivables and/or inventory grow much faster than revenue.**
- **Cumulative CFO over 5–10 years is far below cumulative PAT** (e.g. under 50%).

### Why it happens
| Innocent reasons | Dangerous reasons |
|---|---|
| Rapid growth needing more working capital | **Aggressive revenue recognition** (booking sales before they're earned) |
| A big contract with delayed payment terms | **Channel stuffing** (pushing excess stock to dealers to book sales) |
| Seasonal timing | **Fictitious sales** to related parties or shell companies |
| | Customers unable to pay (bad debts not yet written off) |
| | **Capitalising expenses** to boost profit |

### How to check (5 minutes on Screener)
1. **Cash Flows tab:** add up 10 years of "Cash from Operating Activity".
2. **P&L tab:** add up 10 years of "Net Profit".
3. **Ratio:** cumulative CFO / cumulative PAT. **≥ 90–100% is healthy. < 60% demands investigation.**
4. **Ratios tab:** "Debtor Days" and "Working Capital Days" rising year after year → a warning.

### Lesson from history
**Satyam Computer Services (2009):** the founder confessed that about ₹5,000+ crore of reported cash and bank balances didn't exist, and that profits had been inflated for years. Red flags in hindsight: a huge cash pile that earned unusually low interest, and a large unexplained "debtors" figure. **Cash that doesn't produce interest income, and profits that don't produce cash, are classic warning signs.**

## 4. Red flag #3: Frequent equity dilution

### What it is
The company **repeatedly issues new shares**, through:
- **QIP (Qualified Institutional Placement)**
- **Preferential allotment** (often to promoters or select investors, sometimes at favourable prices)
- **Rights issues**
- **Large ESOP grants**
- **Conversion of warrants or convertible bonds**
- **Issuing shares to pay for acquisitions**

### Why it hurts you
- Your **ownership percentage shrinks**. The same profit is split among more shares, so **EPS grows slower than profit**, or falls.
- **Repeated** dilution suggests the business **can't fund itself** (negative FCF, high debt).
- Preferential allotments to promoters at low prices transfer value from you to them.

### How to check
- **Screener Balance Sheet → "Equity Capital":** if it rises over the years (excluding bonus issues and stock splits, where face value or share count changes but value doesn't), new shares are being issued.
- **Number of shares** over 10 years: has it grown materially?
- **Compare profit growth with EPS growth.** A large gap means dilution.

### Not all issuance is bad
A **one-time** raise to fund a clearly high-return expansion, or by a young company in a growth phase, can be fine. The red flag is **habitual** dilution by a mature company.

## 5. Governance and management red flags

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Auditor resigns mid-term**, especially citing lack of information or concerns | Often a precursor to accounting problems |
| **Qualified audit opinion** or "emphasis of matter" | The auditor has reservations about the numbers |
| **Frequent auditor changes** | Possibly opinion shopping |
| **Small, unknown auditor** for a large company | Less scrutiny |
| **Large related-party transactions**: sales to, purchases from, loans to, or royalties to promoter entities | A route for siphoning money out of the listed company |
| **Promoter steadily reducing stake** | Insiders may know something |
| **Excessive promoter or management pay** relative to profits | Misaligned priorities |
| **Key management or independent director resignations** | Possible internal problems |
| **Regulatory actions:** SEBI orders, tax raids, ED investigations | Serious legal and reputational risk |
| **Complex group structure:** dozens of subsidiaries, cross-holdings, offshore entities | Makes money flows hard to trace |
| **Frequent changes in business "story"** (new hot sector every year) | Management chasing narratives rather than building a business |
| **Over-promising in concalls**, with targets regularly missed | Low credibility |

---

## 6. Accounting red flags

| Red flag | What it might mean |
|---|---|
| **Receivables growing faster than sales** | Aggressive revenue recognition, or collection problems |
| **Inventory growing faster than sales** | Unsold stock, obsolescence, or inflated inventory |
| **Large and rising "other income"** | The core business is weak. Profits are propped up. |
| **Recurring "exceptional" items** | Regular costs disguised as one-offs |
| **Capitalising expenses** (rising intangibles, "capitalised R&D", or CWIP with no new capacity) | Inflating current profit |
| **Changes in depreciation policy** (longer asset lives) | Boosting profit without operational improvement |
| **Unusually low tax rate** without an explanation | Unsustainable, or questionable |
| **Large contingent liabilities** (more than 20–30% of net worth) | Hidden risks: lawsuits, tax disputes, guarantees |
| **Big cash balance + big borrowings + low interest income** | Is the cash real? Is it restricted? |
| **Frequent restatements** of past numbers | Unreliable accounting |
| **Loans and advances to related parties** | Money leaving the company |

---

## 7. Balance sheet and capital allocation red flags

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Rising debt with no rise in revenue or profit** | Borrowing to survive, not to grow |
| **Interest coverage falling below 2–3×** | Difficulty servicing debt in any downturn |
| **Short-term debt funding long-term assets** | Refinancing risk |
| **Large goodwill** from expensive acquisitions, then **impairments** | Overpaid for acquisitions |
| **Unrelated diversification** (a textile company starting real estate, an auto parts maker buying a hotel) | Empire building, dilution of focus |
| **CWIP stuck for years** | Failed or delayed projects. Money potentially parked. |
| **Dividends paid while FCF is negative and debt rises** | Unsustainable, possibly to keep up appearances |
| **Credit rating downgrades** | Independent warning from analysts with access to management |

---

## 8. Business red flags

- **Declining market share** or margins over several years
- **Dependence on one customer, one product or one government contract**
- **Industry under structural disruption**
- **Commoditised products** with no pricing power, in an industry with constant price wars
- **Regulatory risk** that could change the business overnight
- **"Story stocks"**: valuation driven by a narrative (EV, AI, green energy, defence) with little revenue or profit to show

## 9. Lessons from Indian blow-ups (summary)

| Company / group | What happened | Signs that were visible |
|---|---|---|
| **Satyam (2009)** | Fictitious cash and inflated profits for years | A huge cash pile with low interest income; large receivables |
| **Kingfisher Airlines (2012)** | Collapse under massive debt | Chronic losses, rising debt, negative net worth, unpaid salaries |
| **IL&FS (2018)** | AAA-rated infrastructure lender defaulted | Very high leverage, complex web of 300+ subsidiaries, short-term borrowing for long-term projects |
| **DHFL (2019)** | Housing finance company default and fraud allegations | Rapid loan growth, related-party lending, high leverage, promoter pledging |
| **Yes Bank (2020)** | Bank collapse and RBI moratorium | Rapid loan book growth, divergence in NPA reporting flagged by RBI, promoter selling and pledging, CEO term curtailed by RBI |
| **Jet Airways (2019)** | Grounded | Persistent losses, high debt, negative net worth |
| **Various small/mid caps** | Promoter pledges triggered collapses | Rising pledged %, falling promoter holding |

In almost every case, **several red flags** from this chapter were visible in public data **before** the collapse.

## 11. Summary
- **The big three:** **promoter pledging**, **profits without cash flow**, **frequent equity dilution**.
- Also watch **governance** (auditor issues, related-party transactions, promoter selling), **accounting** (rising receivables, other income, exceptional items, contingent liabilities), **balance sheet** (rising debt, goodwill, stuck CWIP) and **business** (losing share, disruption, story stocks).
- Most Indian blow-ups showed **multiple red flags years in advance**.
- One red flag means investigate. Several means **walk away**.
