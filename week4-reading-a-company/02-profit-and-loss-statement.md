## 1. What the P&L shows

The **Profit & Loss statement** (also called the **income statement** or **statement of profit and loss**) shows a company's **revenues, expenses and profit over a period**: a quarter or a financial year.

Think of it as a **video** of the year's business activity (unlike the balance sheet, which is a **photograph** at one moment).

Key questions it answers:
- How much did the company **sell**?
- What did it **cost** to make and sell those products?
- How much **profit** was left for shareholders?
- Is profit **growing** and are **margins** improving?

## 3. Each line explained

### 3.1 Revenue from operations (sales, "top line")
- Money earned from the **core business**: selling products or services.
- Reported **net of GST** and after discounts and returns.
- **Revenue growth** is the first thing to check (Chapter 4.5).
- Break it down where possible: by **segment** (decorative vs industrial paints), **geography** and **volume vs price** (did they sell more litres, or just raise prices?). Volume growth is healthier.

### 3.2 Other income
- Income **not from the core business**: interest on cash and FDs, dividends, gains on selling investments, rental income, foreign exchange gains.
- **Watch out:** if other income is a large or growing share of profit, the core business may be weaker than the headline profit suggests. A company earning ₹500 Cr profit, of which ₹300 Cr is treasury interest, has a weak operating business.

### 3.3 Operating expenses
| Expense | What it includes | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost of materials consumed / COGS** | Raw materials, plus purchases of traded goods, plus changes in inventory | Raw material prices (e.g. crude oil for paints) can squeeze margins |
| **Employee benefit expense** | Salaries, wages, PF, gratuity, ESOP costs | Heavy for IT and services companies (often 50–60% of revenue) |
| **Other expenses** | Advertising, freight, power and fuel, rent, repairs, royalty, legal, travel | Big swings deserve an explanation |

### 3.4 EBITDA: Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation
```
EBITDA = Revenue − Operating expenses (materials + employees + other expenses)
```
- The **profit from core operations**, before financing decisions (interest), accounting for asset wear (depreciation) and tax.
- Useful for comparing **operational efficiency** between companies with different debt levels and asset ages.
- **EBITDA margin (Operating Profit Margin, OPM)** = EBITDA / Revenue = 2,000 / 10,000 = **20%**.
- Screener shows this as "Operating Profit" and "OPM %".

**Caution:** EBITDA ignores capex and interest, which are real costs. Companies that heavily promote "EBITDA" (and not profit or cash flow) may be hiding weak economics. Warren Buffett's line: *"Does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?"*

### 3.5 Depreciation and amortisation (D&A)
- **Depreciation:** spreading the cost of a **tangible** asset (machines, buildings, vehicles) over its useful life. A ₹1,000 Cr plant with a 20-year life → about ₹50 Cr depreciation a year.
- **Amortisation:** the same for **intangible** assets (software, licences, acquired brands, patents).
- It's a **non-cash expense**: no money leaves the bank this year (the cash went out when the asset was bought). That's why it's added back in the cash flow statement (Chapter 4.4).
- **Rising depreciation** usually means the company recently invested in new capacity.

### 3.6 EBIT: Earnings Before Interest and Tax
```
EBIT = EBITDA − D&A + Other income
```
- Profit from the business **before** the effect of how it's financed (debt vs equity) and tax.
- Used in **ROCE** and **interest coverage** (Chapter 4.5).

### 3.7 Finance costs (interest)
- Interest on loans, bonds and lease liabilities.
- **High interest relative to EBIT = financial risk.** If EBIT falls, interest still has to be paid.

### 3.8 Exceptional items
- **One-time** gains or losses: selling a division, a legal settlement, impairment (write-down) of assets, restructuring costs.
- **Adjust for them** when looking at the trend. A one-time gain inflates that year's profit. A one-time loss depresses it.
- **Red flag:** "exceptional" losses that appear **almost every year** aren't exceptional. They're part of the business.

### 3.9 Profit before tax (PBT) and tax
- **Tax expense** = current tax (payable this year) + deferred tax (timing differences between accounting and tax rules).
- Indian corporate tax: about **25.2%** under the concessional regime (Section 115BAA), or higher under the old regime.
- **Effective tax rate** = tax / PBT = 450 / 1,800 = 25%. An unusually low rate deserves a question: tax holidays, SEZ benefits or past losses can end.

### 3.10 PAT: Profit after tax (net profit, "bottom line")
- What's left for **shareholders**: either paid as dividends or retained to grow the business.
- **Net profit margin** = PAT / Revenue = 1,350 / 10,000 = **13.5%**.
- In consolidated statements, look at profit **attributable to owners of the parent**, after **minority interest** (the share of subsidiaries' profits belonging to other shareholders).

### 3.11 EPS: Earnings per share
```
EPS = Net profit attributable to shareholders / Number of shares outstanding
```
- ABC Paints: 1,350 / 100 = **₹13.50**.
- **Diluted EPS** accounts for shares that may be issued (ESOPs, convertibles). It's slightly lower.
- EPS is the "E" in the P/E ratio (Chapter 4.5).
- **EPS growth** can differ from profit growth if the share count changes (buybacks raise EPS; dilution lowers it).

## 4. The profit ladder: a visual summary

```
Revenue                          10,000   (100%)
 − Materials, employees, other   (8,000)
= EBITDA (Operating profit)       2,000   (20.0%)   ← core business profitability
 − Depreciation                    (300)
 + Other income                     150
= EBIT                            1,850   (18.5%)   ← before financing & tax
 − Interest                         (50)
= PBT                             1,800   (18.0%)
 − Tax                             (450)
= PAT (Net profit)                1,350   (13.5%)   ← shareholders' profit
÷ Shares (100 Cr)
= EPS                            ₹13.50
```

## 5. Margins: the profitability lens

| Margin | Formula | ABC Paints | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gross margin** | (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue | (10,000 − 5,500)/10,000 = **45%** | Pricing power over raw material costs |
| **Operating (EBITDA) margin** | EBITDA / Revenue | **20%** | Core operating efficiency |
| **Net profit margin** | PAT / Revenue | **13.5%** | Overall profitability |

### How to use margins
- **Trend over 5–10 years:** stable or rising margins suggest pricing power and a moat. Falling margins suggest competition or cost pressure.
- **Compare with peers:** margins vary hugely by industry.
  - Software/IT services: 20–30% OPM
  - FMCG / consumer brands: 15–25%
  - Paints: 15–22%
  - Retail / supermarkets: 5–9% (low margin, high volume)
  - Commodity businesses (steel, cement): volatile, 10–25% depending on the cycle
- **A low margin isn't necessarily bad.** D-Mart has thin margins but turns over its inventory and assets very fast, which leads to high returns on capital.

## 6. Reading a P&L over time

**Never judge one year.** Look at **10 years** (annual) and the **last 8–12 quarters** for recent trends.

### ABC Paints: a 5-year view (₹ crore)
| | FY-4 | FY-3 | FY-2 | FY-1 | FY (latest) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 6,000 | 6,700 | 7,600 | 8,800 | 10,000 |
| EBITDA | 1,140 | 1,270 | 1,400 | 1,700 | 2,000 |
| OPM % | 19.0% | 19.0% | 18.4% | 19.3% | 20.0% |
| PAT | 750 | 840 | 920 | 1,150 | 1,350 |
| EPS (₹) | 7.50 | 8.40 | 9.20 | 11.50 | 13.50 |

**Reading:**
- Revenue grew from 6,000 to 10,000 in 4 years → **CAGR ≈ 13.6%**.
- PAT grew from 750 to 1,350 → **CAGR ≈ 15.8%**, faster than revenue, so margins expanded slightly.
- OPM is **stable at 18–20%**, which is a sign of pricing power.
- Share count is unchanged (EPS grew in line with PAT), so there's **no dilution**.

### Quarterly results: seasonality
Many businesses are seasonal (paints sell more before Diwali; air conditioners in summer). Compare a quarter with **the same quarter last year (YoY)**, not the previous quarter (QoQ).

## 7. Common P&L tricks and warning signs

| Warning sign | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Revenue growing fast but **receivables growing faster** | Sales may be booked aggressively, or customers aren't paying (check the cash flow) |
| **Profit growing but operating cash flow flat or falling** | Profit may not be "real" cash (Chapter 4.4) |
| Large or rising **other income** | The core business is weaker than PAT suggests |
| Recurring **"exceptional" items** | Hiding regular costs |
| **Capitalising expenses** (treating costs as assets) | Inflates profit now, depreciation later |
| Sudden **drop in the tax rate** | Possibly temporary. Check why. |
| Change in **accounting policies** or **auditors** | Can change how profit is reported |
| **Revenue from related parties** | Could be inflated or not at arm's length |

---

## 8. Summary
- The P&L shows **revenue, expenses and profit over a period**.
- The ladder: **Revenue → EBITDA → EBIT → PBT → PAT → EPS.**
- Key margins: **gross, operating (OPM) and net**. Track the trends and compare with peers.
- Separate **core operations** from **other income** and **exceptional items**.
- Look at **5–10 years** and YoY quarters. Check that EPS grows with profit (no dilution).
- A P&L alone can mislead. Always cross-check with the **cash flow statement**.

2. What's the OPM in question 1?
3. Why is depreciation called a non-cash expense?
4. Why should you be cautious if other income is a large part of profit?
5. Why compare a quarter with the same quarter last year rather than the previous quarter?
