# Chapter 4.4: The Cash Flow Statement: "Where Did the Cash Actually Go?"

> *Based on Varsity's Fundamental Analysis module (the cash flow chapter).*
>
> **What you'll learn:** why profit and cash differ, the three sections of the cash flow statement (operating, investing, financing), how operating cash flow is derived from profit (the indirect method), free cash flow, the "CFO vs PAT" test, the six typical cash flow patterns and what they say about a company, and how the three statements connect.

---

## 1. Why a separate cash flow statement?

**Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.**

The P&L uses **accrual accounting**: revenue is recorded when a sale is made, not when cash is received, and expenses are recorded when incurred, not when paid. That means a company can:
- report a **profit** while its **bank balance shrinks** (customers haven't paid, inventory is piling up)
- report a **loss** while generating cash (large non-cash depreciation)

**Companies don't go bankrupt from losses. They go bankrupt from running out of cash.**

The **cash flow statement** tracks the actual movement of cash in and out of the company during the year, in three buckets.

---

## 2. The three sections

| Section | What it captures | Typical sign for a healthy company |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash flow from operating activities (CFO)** | Cash generated by the **core business**: collecting from customers, paying suppliers, employees and taxes | **Positive** and growing |
| **Cash flow from investing activities (CFI)** | Buying or selling **long-term assets and investments**: capex (plants, machines), acquisitions, buying or selling investments, interest and dividends received | Usually **negative** (investing for growth) |
| **Cash flow from financing activities (CFF)** | Transactions with **lenders and shareholders**: borrowing, repaying debt, issuing shares, buybacks, paying dividends and interest | **Negative** for mature companies (repaying debt, paying dividends). Positive when raising money. |

```
Opening cash + CFO + CFI + CFF = Closing cash
```

## 3. ABC Paints Ltd: cash flow statement (₹ crore)

### A. Cash flow from operating activities (indirect method)
| Item | Amount | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Profit before tax | 1,800 | Start from the P&L |
| + Depreciation & amortisation | 300 | Non-cash expense, so add back |
| + Finance costs | 50 | Moved to the financing section |
| − Other income (interest, dividends) | (150) | Moved to the investing section |
| **= Operating profit before working capital changes** | **2,000** | ≈ EBITDA |
| − Increase in inventories | (100) | More cash tied up in stock |
| − Increase in trade receivables | (100) | Sales not yet collected |
| + Increase in trade payables | 150 | Suppliers not yet paid (cash retained) |
| **= Cash generated from operations** | **1,950** | |
| − Income taxes paid | (450) | |
| **= Net cash from operating activities (CFO)** | **1,500** | |

### B. Cash flow from investing activities
| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase of property, plant & equipment (**capex**) | (800) |
| Purchase of investments (net) | (250) |
| Interest and dividends received | 150 |
| **= Net cash used in investing activities (CFI)** | **(900)** |

### C. Cash flow from financing activities
| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Proceeds from borrowings (net) | 140 |
| Dividends paid (₹5.40 × 100 Cr shares) | (540) |
| Interest paid | (50) |
| **= Net cash used in financing activities (CFF)** | **(450)** |

### Net change in cash
| | |
|---|---|
| CFO + CFI + CFF | 1,500 − 900 − 450 = **+150** |
| Opening cash | 450 |
| **Closing cash** (matches the balance sheet) | **600** |

## 4. Understanding the working capital adjustments

This is the part people find confusing. The logic:
| Change in a balance sheet item | Effect on cash | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Increase in an asset** (inventory, receivables) | **Reduces** cash | Cash was spent on stock, or sales weren't collected |
| **Decrease in an asset** | **Increases** cash | Stock sold off, or old dues collected |
| **Increase in a liability** (payables, customer advances) | **Increases** cash | You kept the cash by not paying yet, or got paid in advance |
| **Decrease in a liability** | **Reduces** cash | You paid suppliers |

**Example:** if ABC's receivables rose by ₹100 Cr, it means ₹100 Cr of this year's "revenue" is still sitting with dealers as dues. It's in the P&L profit but **not** in the bank.

## 5. Free cash flow (FCF)

```
Free cash flow = Cash from operations (CFO) − Capital expenditure (capex)
ABC Paints: 1,500 − 800 = ₹700 crore
```

FCF is the cash left after the company has **maintained and grown its asset base**. It's genuinely "free" to:
- pay **dividends**
- **buy back** shares
- **repay debt**
- make **acquisitions**
- build up cash reserves

### Why FCF matters
- **Valuation:** a business is ultimately worth the cash it can generate for its owners over its life. (That's the basis of DCF valuation, which these notes skip.)
- **Self-funding growth:** positive FCF means the company can grow **without** borrowing or issuing new shares.
- **Dividend sustainability:** dividends paid from FCF are sustainable. Dividends paid from borrowing are not.

**ABC Paints:** FCF ₹700 Cr vs dividends ₹540 Cr → dividends are comfortably covered by free cash.

### Interpreting negative FCF
- **Temporary and productive:** a company in a big expansion phase (a new plant) may have negative FCF for 2–3 years, which is fine if the new capacity later produces revenue and cash.
- **Persistent:** negative FCF year after year, funded by debt or new shares, means the business consumes more cash than it produces. That's a major warning sign.
- **Maintenance vs growth capex:** some capex just keeps existing assets running (maintenance). Rough estimate: maintenance capex ≈ depreciation. FCF after only maintenance capex shows the underlying cash-earning power.

### FCF yield
```
FCF yield = FCF / Market capitalisation
ABC: 700 / 54,000 ≈ 1.3%
```
It shows how much free cash you get per rupee of market value. Higher = cheaper (other things equal).

## 6. The most important test: CFO vs PAT

**Compare operating cash flow (CFO) with net profit (PAT) over 5–10 years.**

```
Cash conversion = Cumulative CFO / Cumulative PAT
```
| Ratio | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **≥ 90–100%** | Profits are turning into cash. **Healthy.** (It can exceed 100% because depreciation is added back.) |
| **60–90%** | Some cash stuck in working capital. Investigate. |
| **< 50%, persistently** | **Red flag.** Profits are largely on paper. Possible aggressive revenue recognition, ballooning receivables, or worse. |

**ABC Paints (latest year):** 1,500 / 1,350 = **111%**. Healthy.

### A famous warning pattern
Many accounting frauds and blow-ups showed the same pattern for years before collapsing: **reported profits rising steadily while CFO stayed weak or negative** and receivables kept growing. Checking CFO vs PAT over 5–10 years is one of the simplest and most powerful red-flag tests available to a retail investor.

## 7. Cash flow patterns: what the signs say

| CFO | CFI | CFF | Typical interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **+** | **−** | **−** | **Mature, healthy company:** generating cash, investing in growth, repaying debt or paying dividends. (ABC Paints, most quality companies.) |
| **+** | **−** | **+** | **Growing company:** operations generate cash, but it's also borrowing or raising equity to fund large expansion. Fine if the investment is productive. |
| **+** | **+** | **−** | Selling assets or investments to repay debt or pay shareholders. Could be restructuring, or simply excess cash returning. Check why. |
| **−** | **−** | **+** | **Startup or troubled company:** operations burn cash, and it's investing while funding everything via debt or equity. Common for young companies. A **red flag** for mature ones. |
| **−** | **+** | **+** | **Distress:** burning cash, selling assets, *and* borrowing. Dangerous. |
| **−** | **+** | **−** | Selling assets to repay lenders while operations burn cash. Possible distress. |

## 8. How the three statements connect

```
            P&L                         BALANCE SHEET                     CASH FLOW
┌──────────────────────┐        ┌──────────────────────────┐      ┌───────────────────────┐
│ Revenue              │        │ Receivables, Inventory,  │◄────►│ Working capital       │
│ − Expenses           │        │ Payables                 │      │ changes (in CFO)      │
│ − Depreciation ──────┼──────► │ Fixed assets (net)  ◄────┼──────┤ Capex (in CFI)        │
│ − Interest           │        │ Borrowings  ◄────────────┼──────┤ Borrow/repay (in CFF) │
│ = PAT ───────────────┼──────► │ Reserves (+PAT − div) ◄──┼──────┤ Dividends (in CFF)    │
└──────────────────────┘        │ Cash  ◄──────────────────┼──────┤ Net change in cash    │
                                └──────────────────────────┘      └───────────────────────┘
```

- **PAT** (P&L) → increases **reserves** (balance sheet) → is the starting point for **CFO** (cash flow).
- **Depreciation** (P&L) reduces **fixed assets** (balance sheet) and is **added back** in CFO.
- **Capex** (CFI) increases **fixed assets / CWIP**.
- **Borrowing / repayment** (CFF) changes **borrowings** (balance sheet).
- **Dividends** (CFF) reduce **reserves**.
- **Net change in cash** (cash flow) = change in **cash** on the balance sheet.

Understanding these links lets you spot inconsistencies. For example, reserves that don't grow by PAT minus dividends, or a cash balance that doesn't match.

---

## 9. Summary
- The cash flow statement shows **actual cash movement** in three parts: **CFO** (operations), **CFI** (investments and capex), **CFF** (debt, equity, dividends).
- CFO starts from profit and adjusts for **non-cash items** and **working capital changes**.
- **FCF = CFO − capex**: the cash available to owners. Persistent negative FCF is a warning.
- **The key test: cumulative CFO ≈ cumulative PAT over 5–10 years.** If CFO lags badly, be suspicious.
- The sign pattern (+/−/−) reveals the company's stage and health.
- The three statements are **linked**. Use the links to check consistency.

## Self-check questions
1. A company reports PAT of ₹500 Cr but CFO of ₹100 Cr. Name two possible reasons.
2. Why is depreciation added back when computing CFO?
3. CFO = ₹800 Cr, capex = ₹1,100 Cr. What's FCF, and is it necessarily bad?
4. What does a cash flow pattern of CFO −, CFI +, CFF + suggest?
5. If trade payables increased by ₹200 Cr, does that increase or decrease CFO? Why?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Receivables or inventory increased sharply (cash stuck in working capital); aggressive or fictitious revenue recognition; large non-cash income.
2. It's a non-cash expense that reduced profit without any cash outflow that year.
3. −₹300 Cr. Not necessarily bad if it's a temporary, productive expansion. Bad if it's persistent and funded by debt or dilution.
4. Distress: operations burn cash, the company is selling assets and borrowing to survive.
5. It increases CFO. The company kept cash by not yet paying suppliers.
</details>
