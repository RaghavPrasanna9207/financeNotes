# Behavioral Finance: The Psychology of Money (Housel's 20 Flaws)

> *Notes on Morgan Housel's original 2018 essay, **"The Psychology of Money: Timeless lessons on wealth, greed, and happiness"**, on the Collaborative Fund blog. His bestselling 2020 book grew out of this essay. Each point below has: **the idea**, **Housel's example**, and **what it means for you** as a young Indian investor.*

---

## Why behavior matters more than knowledge

Housel's central claim: **financial success depends less on what you know and more on how you behave.** You can know everything in Weeks 1–4 of these notes and still lose money by panic-selling in a crash, chasing hot themes, or spending to impress people.

Behavior is hard to teach because it's shaped by ego, fear, greed, personal history and social pressure. The 20 flaws below are the most common ways those forces lead smart people astray.

---

## 1. Earned success and deserved failure fallacy
**The idea:** we underestimate how much **luck and risk** shape outcomes. Housel calls them "two sides of the same coin": both are forces outside our control that make outcomes differ from our decisions.

**Housel's example:** when economist Robert Shiller was asked what he wished he knew about investing, he answered: *"The exact role of luck in successful outcomes."*

**What it means for you:**
- Don't assume a successful investor, trader or fund manager is a genius. They may have been lucky. Don't copy individuals; learn from **broad patterns** that work across many people.
- Don't judge your own bad outcomes too harshly. A good decision can have a bad result.
- Be humble when things go well. Your market-beating year might be luck.

---

## 2. Cost avoidance syndrome
**The idea:** every reward has a **price**. In investing, the price isn't a fee you can see. It's **volatility, fear, doubt and regret**. People try to get the reward without paying the price, and end up with neither.

**Housel's example:** Monster Beverage rose about **211,000%** between 1995 and 2016, but it **fell more than 50% five separate times** along the way. Housel quotes Scott Adams: *"If you want success, figure out the price, then pay it."*

**What it means for you:**
- Equity's ~12% long-run return comes with 20–40% falls every few years. **Volatility is the admission fee, not a fine.**
- Investors who try to avoid the volatility (selling before crashes, waiting for dips) usually miss the returns too.
- Accept upfront that your SIP portfolio will be deeply red at some point. That's the price.

---

## 3. Rich man in the car paradox
**The idea:** people buy expensive things hoping others will admire them. But when you see someone in a nice car, you rarely think "what a great person". You imagine **yourself** in that car. The owner becomes a benchmark for *your* desires.

**What it means for you:**
- Respect and admiration come from **humility, kindness, intelligence and empathy**, not possessions.
- A big chunk of spending (the latest iPhone, a luxury car on EMI, an expensive wedding) is aimed at an audience that isn't really paying attention.
- Spending less on status is one of the easiest ways to raise your savings rate.

---

## 4. A tendency to adjust to current circumstances
**The idea:** your goals and preferences **change** over time, often in ways you can't predict. A plan that fits you at 22 may not fit at 35. When people change course drastically, they **interrupt compounding**.

**What it means for you:**
- Avoid extreme plans ("I'll live on nothing and retire at 35", "100% small caps").
- Build in **balance and room for error**, so the plan survives your future self's changing priorities.
- The **first rule of compounding: never interrupt it unnecessarily.**

---

## 5. Anchored-to-your-own-history bias
**The idea:** your **personal experience** shapes your beliefs about money far more than it should. Someone who started investing in a booming market believes stocks always go up. Someone who lived through a crash believes they're dangerous. Both are extrapolating from a tiny sample.

**Housel's example:** economists found that *"current [investment] beliefs depend on the realizations experienced in the past."* People who grew up during high inflation or crashes invest very differently for the rest of their lives.

**What it means for you:**
- If you started investing after 2020, you've mostly seen a strong Indian bull market. **That isn't normal.** Long flat or falling periods also happen (e.g. 2008–2013 for many stocks).
- Your parents' preference for FDs, gold and real estate comes from *their* history (high FD rates, real estate booms). It's not necessarily right for your 35-year horizon.
- Read history widely to calibrate your expectations beyond your own experience.

---

## 6. Historians are prophets fallacy
**The idea:** finance **changes constantly**: new instruments, new rules, new structures. So **past data is a weaker guide to the future** than people assume.

**Housel's example:** the 401(k), venture capital and the S&P 500's sector composition have all changed dramatically within living memory. Bill Bonner described Mr. Market as having *"a sledgehammer in his hand"*.

**What it means for you:**
- Use history for **general lessons** (markets recover, diversification helps, costs matter), not precise predictions ("the Nifty will give exactly 12%").
- India's market has changed hugely too: SIP flows, T+1, index funds, retail participation and sector weights (IT and financials barely existed in the index decades ago).
- Plan with **ranges**, not point forecasts.

---

## 7. The seduction of pessimism in a world where optimism is the most reasonable stance
**The idea:** pessimism **sounds smarter** and gets more attention ("the crash is coming"). Optimism sounds naive. But over long periods, optimism has been the more accurate stance. Real optimism isn't believing everything will be great. It's recognising that **most people wake up trying to make things better**, so progress compounds even through setbacks.

**Housel's example:** Hans Rosling: *"I am not an optimist. I am a very serious possibilist."*

**What it means for you:**
- Doom headlines and "crash prediction" YouTube videos are entertaining but terrible guides to action.
- Be **pessimistic about the short term** (expect crashes, keep an emergency fund) and **optimistic about the long term** (stay invested for decades).

---

## 8. Underappreciating the power of compounding
**The idea:** humans think **linearly**, so exponential growth always surprises us. We underestimate how big things get over long periods.

**Housel's example:** computer storage went from megabytes to terabytes within decades. Albert Bartlett: *"The greatest shortcoming of the human race is our inability to understand the exponential function."* (In the book, Housel notes that most of Warren Buffett's wealth came after his 60s, because he started young and kept going.)

**What it means for you:**
- Starting at 22 instead of 32 can mean **2–3× more money** at retirement (Chapter 1.1).
- The final years do most of the work. Don't interrupt them.
- Small increases in savings rate or small cuts in costs compound into very large differences.

---

## 9. Attachment to social proof in a field that demands contrarian thinking
**The idea:** following the crowd feels safe, but **above-average returns come from things that aren't yet popular**. When everyone agrees an investment is great, its price usually already reflects that.

**Housel's example:** thousands of people attend Berkshire Hathaway's annual meeting, and nearly all of them consider themselves contrarians.

**What it means for you:**
- When your friends, colleagues and social media are all talking about the same stock, theme or NFO (defence, PSU, EV, smallcaps), it's usually **late**, not early.
- You don't need to be a contrarian picker. **Index funds sidestep this problem entirely.** But at least avoid piling into hype.

---

## 10. An appeal to academia in a field that's governed by loose and unpredictable trends
**The idea:** academic finance offers precise formulas, but markets are driven by **messy human emotions**. Formulas can break down in practice. A plan that's "optimal" on paper but impossible to stick with is worse than a reasonable plan you'll follow.

**Housel's example:** Nobel laureate **Harry Markowitz**, who invented modern portfolio theory, split his own money 50/50 between stocks and bonds to **minimise future regret**: *"I visualized my grief if the stock market went way up and I wasn't in it — or if it went way down and I was completely in it."*

**What it means for you:**
- **Reasonable beats rational.** If 80% equity is "optimal" but you'd panic at a 30% fall, choose 60%.
- Pick an asset allocation for how you'll actually *feel*, not just for a spreadsheet.

---

## 11. The social utility of money coming at the direct expense of growing money
**The idea:** spending money to **show** people you have money is the fastest way to have less money. **Wealth is what you don't see**: the cars not bought, the clothes not worn, the money not spent. Because real wealth is invisible, people struggle to learn from it, and they copy visible spending instead.

**Housel's example:** Rihanna nearly went broke despite enormous earnings, after overspending.

**What it means for you:**
- Your colleague's new car tells you about their **spending**, not their **wealth**. They may have a large loan and no savings.
- Being rich (high income, visible spending) ≠ being wealthy (assets that give freedom).
- Wealth's real value is **options and freedom**: changing jobs, taking a break, handling emergencies.

---

## 12. A tendency toward action in a field where the best decision is often doing nothing
**The idea:** it's hard to tell whether a plan is **broken** or just **temporarily out of favour**. So people tinker, switching funds, timing markets, reacting to news, and interrupt compounding.

**What it means for you:**
- Volatility is a normal part of almost every good plan.
- **"Don't do anything"** may be the most powerful financial advice, and the hardest to follow.
- Review your portfolio **once a year**, not every day. Most "action" is noise.
- Changing an index fund SIP because one active fund beat it last year is a classic example of harmful action.

---

## 13. Underestimating the need for room for error, not just financially but mentally and physically
**The idea:** a **margin of safety** lets you **survive** bad outcomes and stay in the game long enough for compounding to work. Room for error is needed in your **finances** (savings, low debt) and also in your **mind** (how much stress you can handle) and **body** (sleep, health).

**Housel's example:** Benjamin Graham: *"The purpose of the margin of safety is to render the forecast unnecessary."* Housel notes that some investors quit after losses not because they ran out of money, but because they were mentally and physically exhausted.

**What it means for you:**
- **Emergency fund**, adequate **insurance**, **low debt**, and **assumptions below average returns** (plan with 10–11%, not 15%).
- Don't run your finances so tight that one surprise forces you to sell or borrow.
- Choose an asset allocation that lets you **sleep at night**.

---

## 14. A tendency to be influenced by the actions of other people who are playing a different financial game than you are
**The idea:** investors have **different goals and time horizons**. A day trader and a 30-year SIP investor are playing completely different games, but they trade in the same market at the same prices. When long-term investors take cues from short-term traders, they get hurt.

**Housel's example:** **Cisco's stock in 1999** rose to extraordinary valuations because traders expected it to keep rising in the short term, which was rational *for traders*. Long-term investors who bought at those prices because "the price keeps going up" suffered terrible losses when the traders moved on.

**What it means for you:**
- Before acting on any tip, ask: **is this person playing my game?** A trader's "buy now" has nothing to do with your 20-year retirement plan.
- Write down *your* game ("I invest monthly for 25+ years for retirement") and ignore signals from other games: F&O traders, momentum chasers, influencers.

---

## 15. An attachment to financial entertainment
**The idea:** money is emotional, so it attracts **sensational media coverage** and fan-like engagement. Financial news is designed to grab attention, not to improve your decisions.

**Housel's example:** a **3% stock market drop** makes headlines, while a much larger change in something like blood pressure wouldn't make the news at all. *"Boring is good."* Opportunities often lie where others aren't looking.

**What it means for you:**
- Business TV, stock-tip Telegram channels and social media hot takes are **entertainment**. Consuming them often makes outcomes worse, because they trigger action (Flaw #12).
- The best long-term investing (index fund SIPs, annual rebalancing) is **deeply boring**. That's a feature.

---

## 16. Optimism bias in risk-taking, or "Russian Roulette should statistically work" syndrome
**The idea:** an action can have **favourable odds** and still carry a **risk of ruin**: a small chance of a catastrophe you can never recover from. **Leverage** turns ordinary risks into ruinous ones.

**Housel's example:** Nassim Taleb: *"You can be risk loving and yet completely averse to ruin."* In 2009, households and companies with heavy debt were wiped out during the crisis and **couldn't benefit from the recovery** that followed, while unleveraged investors who simply held on recovered fully.

**What it means for you:**
- **Never take a risk that could knock you out of the game permanently**, however good the odds look.
- This is the strongest argument against **F&O trading, margin trading, borrowing to invest, and concentrated bets with money you can't afford to lose**.
- The ability to stay in the game is worth more than any single high return.

---

## 17. A preference for skills in a field where skills matter less than behavior
**The idea:** intelligence, credentials and technical skill are **worth little without the right behavior**. Basic habits (saving, patience, avoiding debt, not panicking) outperform sophisticated skills used badly.

**Housel's example:**
- **Richard Fuscone**, a Harvard MBA and former Merrill Lynch executive, went bankrupt after borrowing heavily to fund an extravagant lifestyle.
- **Grace Groner**, who worked as a secretary, lived frugally, bought a few blue-chip shares and held them for decades. She left **about $7 million** to charity when she died.

**What it means for you:**
- You don't need to be a stock-picking genius. A high savings rate + index funds + time + patience beats most "skilled" approaches.
- Don't let your intelligence talk you into complexity.

---

## 18. Denial of inconsistencies between how you think the world should work and how it actually works
**The idea:** we hold **narratives** about how the economy and markets *should* work, and we filter out information that contradicts them. When evidence conflicts with our story, we dismiss the evidence. **"Stories over statistics."**

**Housel's example:** the relationship between tax rates and economic growth is hard to spot in the data, but people who believe higher taxes must slow growth tend to dismiss evidence that doesn't fit.

**What it means for you:**
- Notice when you reject data because it's inconvenient (e.g. "my active fund manager is different" when SPIVA data says most underperform; "real estate always goes up" when data shows long flat periods).
- Actively look for evidence **against** your favourite investment thesis.

---

## 19. Political beliefs driving financial decisions
**The idea:** partisan views distort how people judge the economy and markets, even when the data doesn't support them. People feel the economy is better or worse depending on who's in power.

**Housel's example:** when Housel published data on economic performance by US president, it upset readers whose politics clashed with the numbers. Around the 2016 election, people's views of the economy flipped overnight even though GDP, jobs and interest rates hadn't materially changed. **Only the president had.**

**What it means for you:**
- Don't sell your index funds because of an election result, or buy a "theme" because of a policy announcement.
- Over decades, markets have grown under governments of every kind. **Keep politics out of your portfolio.**

---

## 20. The three-month bubble
**The idea:** we assume whatever **just happened** will keep happening and affect us just as strongly (recency bias).

**Housel's example:** after 9/11, some media outlets treated another attack as inevitable. After the 2008 crash, many expected another plunge, and "markets can crash and crashes suck" became the dominant takeaway. Many people stayed out of stocks for years and **missed one of the biggest bull markets in history**, even though markets have historically recovered over time.

**What it means for you:**
- After a crash, you'll feel certain another one is coming. After a rally, you'll feel certain it will continue. **Both feelings are recency bias.**
- Your **asset allocation and SIP plan** are designed to work regardless of what just happened. Stick to them.

---

## The big lessons: a summary

| Lesson | Flaws it comes from | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Luck and risk are everywhere** | 1, 5, 6 | Learn from patterns, not individuals. Be humble. |
| **Volatility is the price of returns** | 2, 12, 20 | Pay it. Keep SIPs running in crashes. |
| **Don't interrupt compounding** | 4, 8, 12 | Start early, stay invested, review yearly. |
| **Room for error and avoiding ruin** | 13, 16 | Emergency fund, insurance, low debt, no leverage, conservative assumptions. |
| **Wealth is invisible** | 3, 11 | Spend less on status. Save the difference. |
| **Reasonable beats rational** | 10, 13 | Choose an allocation you can stick with. |
| **Play your own game** | 9, 14, 15 | Ignore traders, tips, hype and financial entertainment. |
| **Behavior beats skill** | 17 | Simple habits, done consistently, for decades. |
| **Watch your stories** | 7, 18, 19 | Long-term optimism. Seek disconfirming evidence. Keep politics out. |

### One-line version
> **Save consistently, invest simply and cheaply, avoid ruin, keep enough room for error to sleep at night, and give compounding decades without interruption.**
