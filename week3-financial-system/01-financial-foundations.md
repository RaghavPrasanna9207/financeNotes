# Chapter 3.1: Financial Foundations: Before You Invest

> **What you'll learn:** the order of operations in personal finance, how to track cash flow and set a savings rate, how to size and hold an emergency fund, how to handle debt (good vs bad debt, credit cards, the avalanche and snowball methods), your credit score, and how to set goals properly.

---

## 1. The order of operations

Investing comes **fourth**, not first. Skipping the earlier steps is how people end up selling investments at the worst time or losing everything to a medical bill.

```
 1. Know your cash flow (income, expenses, savings rate)
          ↓
 2. Build an emergency fund
          ↓
 3. Get insured (health for everyone, term life if you have dependents)
          ↓
 4. Clear high-interest debt (credit cards, personal loans, BNPL)
          ↓
 5. Invest for goals (retirement, house, education) with the right asset allocation
          ↓
 6. Review once a year: rebalance, step up SIPs, update insurance
```

Each step protects the next. An emergency fund protects your investments from forced selling. Insurance protects your emergency fund from being wiped out. Clearing debt stops compounding from working *against* you.

## 2. Step 1: Know your cash flow

### Track for 2–3 months
Write down (or use an app or spreadsheet) where every rupee goes. Group the spending into:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Fixed needs** | Rent, EMIs, insurance premiums, school fees, utilities |
| **Variable needs** | Groceries, transport, medicines |
| **Wants** | Eating out, shopping, subscriptions, travel, gadgets |
| **Savings and investments** | SIPs, PPF, emergency fund contributions |

### Savings rate
```
Savings rate = (Income − Expenses) / Income
```
- Your savings rate is the **biggest driver of wealth** early in your career, more than returns.
- A common guideline: save **at least 20%** of take-home pay, and aim for 30–50% if you can, especially before you have family responsibilities.
- **The 50/30/20 rule** (a rough starting framework): 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings. Adjust to your situation.

### "Pay yourself first"
Set your SIPs and transfers to run **on or right after salary day**. Then spend what's left. If you invest whatever is left *after* spending, there's usually nothing left.

### Lifestyle inflation
As income rises, spending tends to rise just as fast (a better phone, a bigger flat, a car). A powerful habit: **save at least half of every raise**. Your lifestyle still improves, and so does your savings rate.

## 3. Step 2: The emergency fund

### What it's for
Unplanned, urgent expenses or loss of income: job loss, a medical emergency not fully covered by insurance, urgent home or vehicle repairs, a family emergency.

**It's NOT for:** vacations, gadgets, planned purchases or "great investment opportunities".

### How much?
| Situation | Emergency fund |
|---|---|
| Student / no dependents, living with family | 3 months of expenses |
| Salaried, stable job, no dependents | 6 months |
| Salaried with dependents / single income family | 6–9 months |
| Freelancer, business owner, variable income, or volatile industry | 9–12 months |

"Expenses" means **essential monthly outflows including EMIs and insurance premiums**, not your income.

### Where to keep it
The priorities are **safety and liquidity**, not returns.

| Where | Share | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Savings account** | ~1 month's expenses | Instant access |
| **Sweep-in FD** (auto-linked to savings) | Part | Higher interest, breaks automatically when needed |
| **Liquid fund / overnight fund** | The rest | ~6–7% returns, money in your bank in 1 working day. Many offer instant redemption up to ₹50,000. |

**Don't keep it in:** equity funds (could be down 30% when you need it), PPF or long FDs (locked), gold (volatile), real estate (illiquid), or lent to friends.

### Building it
If you don't have one, **build the emergency fund before starting long-term equity SIPs**. If that feels too slow, split new savings, e.g. 70% to the emergency fund and 30% to SIPs, until the fund is complete.

**Refill** it whenever you use it.

## 4. Step 3: Insurance (summary; full detail in Chapter 3.7)
- **Health insurance:** for everyone, separate from employer cover.
- **Term life insurance:** if anyone depends on your income.
- Insurance protects your emergency fund and investments from being wiped out by a single event.

## 5. Step 4: Debt

### Good debt vs bad debt
| | "Productive" debt (can make sense) | "Destructive" debt (avoid or clear fast) |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Home loan (reasonable size), education loan for a degree with clear returns, business loan with a clear plan | Credit card balances, personal loans for consumption, BNPL for gadgets, car loans for expensive cars |
| Typical rate | 8–11% | 14–45% |
| Builds | An asset, or earning capacity | Nothing. The thing is consumed or depreciates. |

### Credit cards: use them, never borrow on them
- Credit cards are excellent tools (rewards, an interest-free period of up to ~50 days, purchase protection) **only if you pay the full statement balance every month**.
- If you pay only the **minimum due** (usually 5%), interest of **3–3.75% per month (36–45% a year)** is charged on the entire outstanding balance, *and* you lose the interest-free period on new purchases.
- Set up **auto-pay for the total amount due**.

### Which debts to pay first?
1. **Avalanche method (mathematically best):** pay minimums on all debts, then put every extra rupee into the **highest-interest** debt first. Once it's cleared, move to the next highest.
2. **Snowball method (psychologically easier):** pay off the **smallest balance** first for quick wins and motivation, then the next smallest.

Either method beats doing nothing. Avalanche saves more interest.

### Prepay a loan or invest?
- **Rate > 10–12% (credit card, personal loan):** pay it off. A guaranteed, tax-free "return" equal to the interest rate beats any investment.
- **Home loan at ~8–9%:** it's a judgment call. Partly prepaying gives a guaranteed return equal to the loan rate. Investing in equity has a higher *expected* return but no guarantee. Many people do both. (Note: under the old regime, home-loan interest gets a tax deduction, which reduces the effective rate.)
- **Education loan:** interest is deductible under the old regime (Section 80E), so the effective cost is lower.

### EMI rule of thumb
Keep **total EMIs below 30–40% of take-home pay**. Lenders may allow more, but it leaves you fragile.

## 6. Credit score

### What it is
A 3-digit number (300–900) from **credit bureaus**: **CIBIL (TransUnion CIBIL), Experian, Equifax, CRIF High Mark**. It summarises your borrowing and repayment history. Banks use it to approve loans and set interest rates.
- **750+** is generally considered good, and gets better rates.

### What affects it
| Factor | Impact |
|---|---|
| **Payment history:** paying EMIs and card bills on time | Biggest factor. Even one 30+ day delay hurts for years. |
| **Credit utilisation:** % of your credit card limit used | Keep it below ~30% |
| **Length of credit history** | Older accounts help. Don't close your oldest card unnecessarily. |
| **Credit mix:** secured (home, auto) + unsecured (cards, personal loans) | A healthy mix helps a little |
| **Number of recent enquiries** | Many loan or card applications in a short time hurt |

### Tips
- Check your score free once a year (each bureau allows a free report).
- A first credit card (even a secured card against an FD) helps a student or new earner build history.
- **Never** co-sign or guarantee loans casually. You're fully liable.

## 7. Setting financial goals

### Make goals SMART
**Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.**
- Vague: "I want to save for a house."
- SMART: "I want ₹25 lakh for a house down payment in 7 years."

### Classify goals by time horizon
| Horizon | Examples | Asset mix (Chapter 3.2) |
|---|---|---|
| Short (< 3 years) | Laptop, bike, travel, emergency fund top-up | Debt / liquid funds / FDs |
| Medium (3–7 years) | Car, higher studies, wedding, house down payment | Hybrid: debt + some equity |
| Long (7+ years) | Retirement, children's education, financial independence | Mostly equity |

### The goal calculation (three steps)
1. **Inflate the cost:** `Future cost = Today's cost × (1 + inflation)^years`
2. **Subtract what you already have** (grown at its expected return)
3. **Find the monthly SIP:** `=PMT(expected_return/12, years*12, 0, -future_cost_remaining, 1)`

**Example:** house down payment, ₹20 L today, 7 years away, 6% inflation.
1. Future cost = 20 L × 1.06^7 = **₹30.1 L**
2. Already saved: nothing.
3. Asset mix about 60% equity / 40% debt, expected return about 10%. Monthly SIP = `PMT(10%/12, 84, 0, -3010000, 1)` ≈ **₹24,700/month**

### Retirement: the biggest goal
- It's usually the **largest** and **longest** goal, and you can't take a loan for retirement.
- A common rough rule: you need a corpus of about **25–33× your annual expenses at retirement** (this corresponds to a 3–4% withdrawal rate). Indian planners often use more conservative multiples because of higher inflation.
- Start retirement saving early. Compounding over 30–35 years does most of the work (Chapter 1.1).
- EPF and NPS are part of retirement savings. Count them.

## 8. Summary

- Order: **cash flow → emergency fund → insurance → clear bad debt → invest → review yearly**.
- Aim to save at least **20%** of take-home pay. **Pay yourself first.** Save half of every raise.
- **Emergency fund:** 3–12 months of expenses in savings + sweep FD + liquid fund. Never in equity.
- **Never carry credit card debt.** Clear high-interest debt before investing (avalanche method).
- Keep your **credit score** 750+ by paying on time and keeping utilisation low.
- Make goals **SMART**, inflate their costs, and match assets to the horizon.

## Self-check questions
1. Why should the emergency fund come before equity investing?
2. Your monthly expenses are ₹40,000 (including a ₹10,000 EMI). You're salaried with no dependents. How big should your emergency fund be, and where should it be held?
3. Explain why paying only the "minimum due" on a credit card is so expensive.
4. What's the difference between the avalanche and snowball methods?
5. A goal costs ₹10 L today and is 5 years away. Inflation is 7%. What's the future cost?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Without it, an emergency could force you to sell equity during a crash, locking in losses.
2. About ₹2.4 L (6 months × ₹40,000): roughly ₹40k in savings and the rest in a sweep FD and liquid fund.
3. Interest of 36–45% a year applies to the entire outstanding balance, and new purchases lose their interest-free period.
4. Avalanche pays the highest-interest debt first (saves the most money). Snowball pays the smallest balance first (quick motivation).
5. 10 L × 1.07^5 ≈ ₹14.0 L.
