# Chapter 3.2: Asset Allocation, Diversification and Rebalancing

> **Core**

> **What you'll learn:** what asset allocation is and why it matters more than fund selection, how to decide your allocation (horizon, risk capacity, risk tolerance), sample portfolios, how diversification really works, rebalancing methods (calendar vs threshold, with worked examples), tax-efficient rebalancing, glide paths for goals, and the mistakes to avoid.

---

## 1. What asset allocation is

**Asset allocation** is how you divide your investments among **asset classes**: equity, debt, gold (and optionally real estate, international equity, cash).

Example: "70% equity, 25% debt, 5% gold."

### Why it's the most important decision
- Each asset class has a very different return and risk profile (Chapter 1.3). The **mix** determines most of your portfolio's long-run return *and* how much it swings along the way.
- A famous study of US pension funds (Brinson, Hood & Beebower, 1986, and follow-ups) found that asset allocation policy explained **over 90% of the variation in a portfolio's returns over time**. Stock selection and market timing explained much less.
- Practical meaning: **whether you're 80% equity or 30% equity matters far more than which flexi-cap fund you chose.**

Asset allocation also drives **behavior**. The right allocation is one whose worst years you can live through without panicking.

## 2. How to decide your allocation

Three inputs:

### Input 1: Time horizon (most important)
When will you need the money? Each **goal** can have its own allocation.

| Time to goal | Equity | Debt | Gold |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 1 year | 0% | 100% (liquid/ultra-short) | 0% |
| 1–3 years | 0–15% | 85–100% | 0% |
| 3–5 years | 20–40% | 60–80% | 0–10% |
| 5–7 years | 40–60% | 35–55% | 5–10% |
| 7–10 years | 60–70% | 25–35% | 5–10% |
| 10+ years | 70–85% | 10–25% | 5–10% |

### Input 2: Risk capacity (can you afford the risk?)
An objective measure of how much loss your finances can absorb.
- **Higher capacity:** stable income, young, no dependents, emergency fund in place, little debt, long horizon.
- **Lower capacity:** unstable income, dependents relying on you, heavy EMIs, a goal coming soon.

### Input 3: Risk tolerance (can you stomach the risk?)
A subjective measure of how you'll *feel* and *act* during a crash.

**The crash test:** at your chosen equity %, imagine equity falls **40%** (it has happened: 2008).
| Equity allocation | Portfolio fall (approx., with debt flat) | On ₹10 L |
|---|---|---|
| 90% | −36% | −₹3.6 L |
| 70% | −28% | −₹2.8 L |
| 50% | −20% | −₹2.0 L |
| 30% | −12% | −₹1.2 L |

**Ask yourself honestly: would you keep your SIPs running and not sell?** If the answer is no, lower the equity allocation. A slightly lower-return plan you stick with beats a "better" plan you abandon at the bottom.

### Rules of thumb (rough starting points only)
- **Equity % = 100 − age** (traditional), or **110/120 − age** (for longer lifespans and higher risk capacity)
  - Age 22 → 78–98% equity. Age 45 → 55–75% equity.
- These ignore your goals, income and temperament. Use them only as a sanity check.

### Count all your assets
- **EPF, PPF, VPF, NPS (debt part), FDs, debt funds** → debt
- **Equity mutual funds, stocks, NPS (equity part), ELSS** → equity
- **Gold ETFs, SGBs, gold funds** → gold (jewellery you won't sell doesn't count)
- **Emergency fund** → keep it **separate** from the investment allocation
- **Home you live in** → exclude it

**Example:** a salaried person has ₹3 L in EPF and ₹7 L in equity funds → 70% equity / 30% debt, even though they never bought a "debt fund".

## 3. Sample allocations

### For long-term goals (retirement, 15+ years)
| Profile | Equity | Debt | Gold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aggressive (young, high tolerance) | 80–90% | 5–15% | 5–10% |
| Moderate | 60–70% | 20–30% | 5–10% |
| Conservative | 40–50% | 40–50% | 5–10% |

### Implementing it simply
| Asset | Simple implementation |
|---|---|
| Equity | Nifty 50 index fund (+ optionally Nifty Next 50 / Midcap 150 / flexi cap / international FoF) |
| Debt | EPF/VPF and PPF first, then a short-duration or corporate bond fund, target maturity fund, or gilt fund |
| Gold | Gold ETF or gold fund |

**Example portfolio for a 25-year-old (aggressive-moderate):**
- 55% Nifty 50 index fund
- 15% Nifty Next 50 or Midcap 150 index fund
- 10% international (S&P 500 FoF), optional
- 15% debt (EPF + PPF)
- 5% gold ETF

## 4. Diversification

### What it is
Spreading money across investments that **don't all fall at the same time**, so one bad outcome can't sink you.

### Levels of diversification
1. **Across asset classes:** equity, debt, gold. The most powerful level, because these assets respond differently to the same events.
   - 2008: Nifty −52% for the calendar year; gold in rupees was up about 25–30%.
   - 2020 crash: equity fell sharply; gold and government bonds rose.
2. **Within equity, across companies and sectors:** a Nifty 50 index fund already holds 50 companies across ~13 sectors.
3. **Across company sizes:** large, mid and small caps behave differently over cycles.
4. **Across countries:** Indian and US markets don't move in lockstep, and a US fund adds **rupee-depreciation protection** (as the rupee falls, foreign assets are worth more in rupees).
5. **Across time:** SIPs spread purchases over many market levels.

### What diversification is NOT
- **Owning many similar funds.** Six large-cap and flexi-cap funds hold mostly the same stocks (overlap). That's **"diworsification"**: more complexity and cost, no real diversification.
- **Owning many stocks in the same sector**, e.g. five private banks.
- **Owning many insurance policies.**

### Correlation is the key
Diversification works only if the assets have **low correlation** (they don't move together). Adding a second Nifty 50 fund adds zero diversification (correlation ≈ 1). Adding gold or high-quality bonds adds a lot.

### The free lunch
Harry Markowitz called diversification **"the only free lunch in finance"**: you can reduce risk without proportionally reducing expected return.

## 5. Rebalancing

### Why portfolios drift
Different assets grow at different rates. After a strong equity rally, your equity share rises above target, so you're taking more risk than you planned. After a crash, it falls below target.

### What rebalancing does
It brings your portfolio **back to the target allocation**. In practice, rebalancing **forces you to sell what has risen (sell high) and buy what has fallen (buy low)**, which is the opposite of what emotions tell you to do.

### Worked example
Target: **60% equity / 40% debt.** Starting value: ₹10 L (₹6 L equity, ₹4 L debt).

**Scenario A: equity rallies 40%, debt grows 7%.**
- Equity: ₹8.4 L, debt: ₹4.28 L. Total: ₹12.68 L.
- Actual allocation: **66% equity / 34% debt.**
- Target equity = 60% × 12.68 = ₹7.61 L → **move about ₹0.79 L from equity to debt.**

**Scenario B: equity crashes 30%, debt grows 7%.**
- Equity: ₹4.2 L, debt: ₹4.28 L. Total: ₹8.48 L.
- Actual allocation: **50% equity / 50% debt.**
- Target equity = 60% × 8.48 = ₹5.09 L → **move about ₹0.89 L from debt into equity.** You're buying equity at crash prices.

### When to rebalance
| Method | How | Pros/cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Calendar** | Once a year on a fixed date (e.g. your birthday, or 1 April) | Simple, disciplined. Might miss big mid-year moves. |
| **Threshold (band)** | Whenever any asset drifts more than **±5 percentage points** from target (e.g. equity above 65% or below 55% on a 60% target) | Responds to big moves. Needs occasional monitoring. |
| **Hybrid** | Check once a year (or at major market events), rebalance only if outside the band | Best of both. **Recommended.** |

**Don't rebalance too often.** Monthly or quarterly rebalancing adds tax, exit loads and effort for little benefit.

### How to rebalance tax-efficiently
In order of preference:
1. **Redirect new money:** send fresh SIPs, bonuses or lump sums to the underweight asset. No selling, so no tax and no exit load. For most young investors with growing SIPs, this alone is usually enough.
2. **Rebalance within tax-advantaged accounts:** e.g. change the NPS equity/debt mix (no tax on switches within NPS).
3. **Use the annual LTCG exemption:** equity LTCG up to **₹1.25 L per financial year is tax-free**. Sell equity units held over 12 months, up to that gain, when you need to trim.
4. **Avoid selling** units held under 12 months (STCG at 20% + exit load).
5. **Hybrid / multi-asset funds** rebalance internally without creating a tax event for you.

## 6. Glide paths: de-risking as goals approach

For a **goal with a fixed date** (a house purchase, a child's college), the risk of a crash *just before* you need the money is serious. A 30% fall a year before the goal could wreck it.

**Glide path:** gradually shift from equity to debt as the goal approaches.

**Example:** child's college in 2038. Starting in 2026, 12 years away.
| Years to goal | Equity | Debt |
|---|---|---|
| 12–7 | 75% | 25% |
| 6 | 65% | 35% |
| 5 | 55% | 45% |
| 4 | 40% | 60% |
| 3 | 25% | 75% |
| 2 | 10% | 90% |
| 1 | 0% | 100% (liquid / short-term debt) |

- Start moving **3–5 years before** the goal.
- If markets fall sharply in the de-risking years, you can **move more slowly** (wait for a recovery), provided you have some flexibility.
- **Retirement** works slightly differently. You'll need money for 25–30 years *after* retiring, so you don't go to 0% equity at retirement. A typical retiree portfolio still holds 30–50% equity, with a "bucket" of 2–3 years' expenses in debt or liquid funds.

## 7. Common mistakes

1. **No allocation at all:** buying whatever is hot this year.
2. **Changing allocation based on recent returns:** going 100% equity after a bull run, 0% after a crash. That's buying high and selling low.
3. **Ignoring EPF/PPF** when calculating allocation (and ending up more conservative or aggressive than you think).
4. **Never rebalancing:** after a long bull run, an intended 60/40 can drift to 80/20, just before a crash.
5. **Too many funds:** overlap without real diversification.
6. **Same allocation for all goals:** a 2-year goal and a 25-year goal need very different mixes.
7. **Treating your house or jewellery as part of your investment portfolio.**

---

## 8. Summary

- **Asset allocation drives most of your outcome.** Fund selection matters less.
- Decide using **time horizon** (first), **risk capacity** and **risk tolerance** (the crash test).
- Count **all** your assets (EPF, PPF, NPS) in the allocation. Keep the emergency fund separate.
- **Diversify across asset classes** with low correlation. Many similar funds ≠ diversification.
- **Rebalance** once a year or when drift is more than 5 points. Prefer redirecting new money, and use the ₹1.25 L LTCG exemption.
- Use a **glide path** for dated goals: start shifting to debt 3–5 years before.
