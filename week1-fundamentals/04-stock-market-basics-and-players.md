## 1. Why invest at all?

Varsity begins with a simple question: **why should you invest?** Three reasons:
1. **To fight inflation.** Money that sits idle loses purchasing power (Chapter 1.2).
2. **To create wealth.** Compounding on equity can turn regular savings into large amounts over decades (Chapter 1.1).
3. **To meet future goals.** Retirement, a house, education. You'll have to *fund* your retirement (~20–30 years without a salary) from savings.

Varsity also suggests a simple exercise: write down your monthly expenses, estimate what you'll need in retirement after inflation, and compare that with what your savings (at FD rates) would produce. For most people the gap is huge, and the only realistic way to close it is to own **equity**.

## 2. Why do stock markets exist?

### The company's side: raising money
Imagine a company that started with the founders' own money, then raised money from angel investors and venture capital, and then took bank loans. To grow further, say by building new factories, it needs much more capital. It has two main options:
- **Borrow (debt):** banks or bonds. Interest must be paid whether business is good or bad.
- **Sell ownership (equity):** issue new shares to the public. No interest. Shareholders share in future profits and losses.

Selling shares to the public for the first time is called an **IPO (Initial Public Offering)**. After the IPO, the company is "listed" on a stock exchange.

### The investor's side: ownership and liquidity
- Investors get to own a part of growing businesses without starting one.
- The most important function of the stock market is **liquidity**: you can sell your shares to another investor any working day. Without an exchange, owning 0.01% of a company would be nearly impossible to sell.
- The market also provides **price discovery**: millions of buyers and sellers continuously agree on what a business is worth.

### Primary market vs secondary market
| | Primary market | Secondary market |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | The company issues **new** shares (IPO, FPO, rights issue) | Investors trade **existing** shares with each other |
| Money goes to | **The company** | **The selling investor** (the company gets nothing) |
| Example | Applying for an IPO through UPI/ASBA | Buying Infosys shares on NSE through your broker app |

When people say "the stock market went up today", they mean the secondary market.

### The IPO process (briefly)
1. The company appoints **merchant bankers** (investment banks) to manage the issue.
2. It files a **DRHP** (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) with SEBI, describing the business, financials, risks and how the money will be used.
3. SEBI reviews it and may ask for changes.
4. A **price band** is set (e.g. ₹480–500) and the issue opens for bidding, usually for 3 days.
5. Investors bid. Retail investors apply through **ASBA/UPI**, so money is blocked in their bank account rather than debited.
6. **Allotment:** if the issue is oversubscribed, shares are allotted by lottery for retail investors.
7. **Listing:** shares start trading on the exchanges, and the **listing price** may be above or below the issue price.

> **A word of caution on IPOs:** IPOs are timed by the company and its bankers to sell at a good price *for the sellers*. Many hyped IPOs list well and then underperform for years. "Listing gains" are a lottery, not an investing strategy.

## 3. The regulator: SEBI

**SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India)** was established in 1988 and given statutory powers in **1992** through the SEBI Act. Its mission:
1. **Protect the interests of investors**
2. **Promote the development of** the securities market
3. **Regulate** the securities market

SEBI regulates:
- Stock exchanges, brokers, depositories, depository participants
- Mutual funds, portfolio managers, investment advisers (RIAs), research analysts
- Listed companies' disclosures, IPOs, takeovers, insider trading
- Foreign portfolio investors

SEBI has quasi-legislative (makes rules), quasi-executive (investigates, inspects) and quasi-judicial (passes orders and penalties) powers. Its famous interventions include the Sahara case, the Satyam fraud action, the 2017 mutual fund categorisation, the upfront margin rules in derivatives, and moving the market to T+1 settlement.

**Investor grievances:** if your broker, a company or a mutual fund doesn't resolve a complaint, file it on **SCORES** (SEBI's complaints portal) or the **SMART ODR** online dispute resolution platform.

## 4. Stock exchanges

A **stock exchange** is a regulated marketplace where buy and sell orders are matched electronically.

### India's two major exchanges
| | **BSE** (Bombay Stock Exchange) | **NSE** (National Stock Exchange) |
|---|---|---|
| Founded | **1875**, Asia's oldest exchange | **1992**, began trading in 1994 |
| Flagship index | **Sensex** (30 stocks) | **Nifty 50** (50 stocks) |
| Notes | More listed companies (many small ones) | Dominant share of equity cash trading volume and almost all derivatives volume |

NSE introduced fully electronic, screen-based trading across India in the 1990s, replacing the open-outcry "trading ring" where brokers shouted orders. Today, all trading is electronic and orders are matched in microseconds.

Most large companies are listed on **both** exchanges. You can buy on either, and prices are almost identical (arbitrageurs eliminate differences).

Other exchanges include **MCX** (commodities), **NCDEX** (agri commodities), **MSE** (Metropolitan Stock Exchange), and **India INX / NSE IX** at GIFT City (international).

## 5. Stock brokers

You **cannot trade directly** on an exchange. Only **SEBI-registered stock brokers** who are **members** of the exchange can place orders.

### Types of brokers
| | Full-service brokers | Discount brokers |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | ICICI Direct, HDFC Securities, Kotak Securities, Motilal Oswal | Zerodha, Groww, Upstox, Angel One, Dhan |
| Services | Research reports, advisory, relationship manager, branch offices | Platform only, self-directed |
| Brokerage | Percentage of trade value (e.g. 0.3–0.5% on delivery) | Flat fee per order (₹0–20). Often ₹0 for equity delivery. |
| Best for | People who want hand-holding (but beware conflicts of interest) | Self-directed investors (most beginners today) |

### What brokers do
- Provide the trading platform (app or website)
- Route orders to the exchange
- Collect and pass on margins and settlement money
- Act as a **depository participant** (opening and maintaining your demat account)
- Send contract notes (trade confirmations) and statements

### Safety features you should know
- Since 2020–2021, brokers **can't misuse client securities** for their own purposes. Pledges need your OTP-based authorisation.
- Client funds must be settled back to your bank regularly ("running account settlement").
- If a broker collapses (as Karvy did in 2019), the regulator and exchanges have mechanisms to recover client securities. The securities sit in **your** demat account, which is why demat is so important.

## 6. Depositories and demat accounts

### Dematerialisation
Before 1996, shares were **physical paper certificates**, which were prone to forgery, theft, loss and painful transfers. The **Depositories Act, 1996** created electronic holding: **dematerialised ("demat") shares**.

### The two depositories
- **NSDL** (National Securities Depository Ltd), set up in 1996
- **CDSL** (Central Depository Services Ltd), set up in 1999. Most discount brokers use CDSL.

A depository is like a **bank for securities**. Just as a bank holds your money in a savings account, a depository holds your shares, bonds, ETFs and SGBs in a **demat account**.

### Depository Participants (DPs)
You don't deal with NSDL or CDSL directly. You open a demat account through a **DP**, usually your broker or bank. Your demat account has a unique 16-digit ID.

### Why this matters to you
- **Your shares are in your name at the depository, not the broker's.** If the broker shuts down, your holdings are safe and can be moved to another DP.
- Every month you get a **CAS (Consolidated Account Statement)** from NSDL or CDSL by email, showing all your demat holdings *and* mutual fund holdings across all brokers and AMCs. Check it regularly. It's your independent source of truth.
- When you **sell**, you authorise the debit with a **TPIN/OTP** (CDSL's eDIS system). This protects you from unauthorised sales.

## 7. Trading account and the three-account setup

To invest in stocks you need three linked accounts:

```
 Bank account  ◄──── money ────►  Trading account  ──── orders ───►  Exchange
                                        │
                                        ▼
                                  Demat account  ◄─── shares credited/debited
```

| Account | Purpose | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Bank account** | Source and destination of money | Your wallet |
| **Trading account** | Place buy/sell orders, holds funds for trading | The shop counter |
| **Demat account** | Holds the securities you own | Your locker |

### Opening an account (KYC)
- **PAN card** (mandatory), **Aadhaar** (address proof, e-sign), bank proof (cancelled cheque or statement), photo, signature.
- **In-person verification** via video (IPV).
- **KYC is centralised:** once done with one SEBI intermediary, it's recorded with a KRA (KYC Registration Agency) and reused.
- **Nomination:** add a nominee (or explicitly opt out). SEBI made this a requirement. Do it, so your family can access your holdings easily.

## 8. Clearing corporations

When you buy shares on an exchange, you don't know who the seller is. What if the seller doesn't deliver the shares, or the buyer doesn't pay?

The **clearing corporation** solves this by becoming the **counterparty to every trade**: the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. This is called **novation**, and it means **settlement is guaranteed**.

- NSE's clearing corporation: **NSE Clearing Ltd** (formerly NSCCL)
- BSE's: **Indian Clearing Corporation Ltd (ICCL)**

They maintain a **Settlement Guarantee Fund**, collect **margins** from brokers and manage defaults.

## 9. Market participants: who's on the other side?

Every share you buy is sold by someone. The main types of participants:

| Participant | Who | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Retail investors** | Individuals investing ≤ ₹2 lakh per IPO application | Growing rapidly. India now has well over 10 crore demat accounts. |
| **HNIs (High Net-worth Individuals)** | Individuals investing larger amounts | Separate IPO category |
| **DIIs (Domestic Institutional Investors)** | Mutual funds, insurance companies (LIC), pension funds (EPFO, NPS), banks | Steady buyers, fuelled by monthly SIP flows |
| **FPIs/FIIs (Foreign Portfolio Investors)** | Foreign funds, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds | Their flows move markets a lot. Heavy FPI selling often causes sharp falls. |
| **Promoters** | Founders and controlling shareholders | Watch their holding and pledging (Week 4) |
| **Traders / arbitrageurs / market makers** | Short-term participants | Provide liquidity |

In recent years, **steady SIP inflows from domestic investors** have become a powerful counterweight to foreign selling, making the Indian market less dependent on FPI moods than it used to be.

## 10. What moves stock prices?

In the short run, prices move with **supply and demand**: more eager buyers than sellers pushes the price up. What drives that eagerness?

**Short term:** news, quarterly results, FPI flows, global markets, interest rate decisions, budget announcements, sentiment, rumours.

**Long term:** the company's **earnings growth** and **cash generation**, and the market's assessment of their sustainability.

Benjamin Graham's famous line: *"In the short run, the market is a voting machine, but in the long run, it is a weighing machine."* Popularity drives short-term prices. Actual business performance determines long-term value.

---

## 11. Summary

- Stock markets let companies raise capital (primary market) and let investors trade ownership with liquidity (secondary market).
- **SEBI** regulates. **NSE/BSE** match orders. **Brokers** connect you. **NSDL/CDSL** hold your shares. **Clearing corporations** guarantee settlement.
- You need a **bank account + trading account + demat account**, opened with KYC. Add a nominee.
- **Your shares are held in your name at the depository**, not by your broker. Check your monthly CAS.
- Prices are driven short-term by sentiment and long-term by earnings.
