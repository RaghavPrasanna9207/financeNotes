# Chapter 1.3: Asset Classes and the Risk–Return Tradeoff

> **What you'll learn:** the four main asset classes (equity, debt, gold, real estate) plus cash, what drives the return of each, the specific risks each carries, how bonds really work (including why bond prices fall when interest rates rise), what "risk" actually means, and how to match assets to time horizons.

---

## 1. What is an asset class?

An **asset class** is a group of investments that share similar characteristics, respond to the same economic forces and are governed by similar rules. Knowing the classes matters because **which asset class you're in determines most of your outcome**. Which particular product you pick within it matters much less.

The main classes for an Indian investor:
1. **Equity:** ownership
2. **Debt (fixed income):** lending
3. **Gold:** a commodity and store of value
4. **Real estate:** property
5. **Cash and cash equivalents:** savings accounts, liquid funds (often grouped with debt)

---

## 2. Equity (shares / stocks)

### What it is
When you buy a share, you buy a **small piece of ownership in a business**. If a company has 1 crore shares and you own 1,000, you own 0.01% of it: of its factories, brands, cash and, most importantly, its **future profits**.

### Where returns come from
Equity returns come from two sources:
1. **Earnings growth:** as the company grows its profits, each share becomes more valuable.
2. **Dividends:** companies share part of their profits with shareholders in cash.
(Plus a third, temporary factor: **valuation change**, i.e. what investors are willing to pay per rupee of earnings, which moves the P/E up or down. In the short run this dominates. In the long run, earnings growth does.)

Over long periods, the stock market's return roughly tracks **nominal GDP growth plus dividends**. India's nominal GDP has grown about 10–12% a year over long periods, which is why long-run Indian equity returns have been around 11–13%.

### Key risks
- **Volatility:** prices swing a lot in the short term. The Nifty 50 has fallen 20%+ many times. Major crashes: 2008 (−60% peak to trough), 2020 COVID (−38% in about a month).
- **Business risk:** an individual company can fail completely (Jet Airways, Satyam, DHFL). Diversification across many companies removes most of this risk.
- **Valuation risk:** buying when the market is very expensive can lead to years of poor returns.

### But over long periods…
Volatility shrinks as the holding period grows. Indian large-cap equity has rarely delivered negative returns over any **7–10 year window**, but it's frequently negative over 1-year windows. **Equity is risky for money you need soon, and is the best wealth builder for money you can leave for a long time.**

### Types within equity
- By size: **large cap** (top 100 companies), **mid cap** (101–250), **small cap** (251+). Smaller means higher growth potential and much higher volatility.
- By geography: Indian vs international (US, global).
- By style: growth, value, dividend.

### How to own it
Direct stocks (needs research, Week 4), **equity mutual funds**, **index funds and ETFs** (simplest, Week 2).

## 3. Debt / fixed income

### What it is
You **lend money** to someone (the government, a bank, a company), and they promise to pay you **interest** and return your **principal** on a set date.

### Common debt instruments in India
| Instrument | Borrower | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Savings account, FD, RD | Bank | DICGC insurance up to ₹5 L per bank |
| PPF, NSC, SCSS, Sukanya Samriddhi | Government (small savings schemes) | Sovereign guarantee, rates reset quarterly |
| EPF | Retirement body (EPFO) | For salaried employees |
| **G-Secs / T-Bills** | Government of India | Zero credit risk. Can be bought via RBI Retail Direct. |
| State Development Loans | State governments | Near-sovereign |
| **Corporate bonds / NCDs** | Companies | Higher yield, credit risk |
| **Debt mutual funds** | Pooled portfolios of the above | Liquid, diversified |

### How a bond works (key vocabulary)
- **Face value:** the amount repaid at maturity (e.g. ₹1,000).
- **Coupon:** the fixed annual interest rate on the face value (e.g. 7%, meaning ₹70 a year).
- **Maturity:** when the principal is repaid.
- **Yield / YTM (Yield to Maturity):** the *actual* return you get if you buy at today's market price and hold to maturity. It differs from the coupon whenever the price differs from face value.

### Why bond prices fall when interest rates rise
This is the single most important concept in debt investing.

Say you own a bond paying a **7% coupon**. The RBI raises rates, and **new** bonds now pay **8%**. Nobody will pay full price for your 7% bond when they can buy an 8% one, so **your bond's price must fall** until its effective yield matches 8%. The reverse is also true: when rates fall, existing higher-coupon bonds become more valuable and their prices **rise**.

```
Interest rates ↑  →  existing bond prices ↓
Interest rates ↓  →  existing bond prices ↑
```

**Duration** measures how sensitive a bond's price is to rate changes. Roughly:
```
% change in price ≈ − Modified duration × change in interest rate
```
A bond fund with a modified duration of **5 years** falls about **5%** if rates rise by 1%. A liquid fund (duration of a few days or weeks) barely moves.
**Longer duration = more interest-rate risk.**

This is why gilt and long-duration funds can have negative years even though they hold only government bonds, and why **liquid and short-duration funds** are used for money you need soon.

### Key risks in debt
1. **Credit (default) risk:** the borrower doesn't pay. Government debt has effectively zero. Corporate debt carries real risk: IL&FS (2018) and DHFL defaulted, and **Franklin Templeton shut six debt funds in 2020** because of illiquid, low-rated papers. Credit ratings (AAA, AA, A, BBB…) from CRISIL, ICRA, CARE and others indicate the risk. AAA is the safest.
2. **Interest-rate risk:** explained above.
3. **Inflation risk:** fixed payments lose real value (Chapter 1.2).
4. **Liquidity risk:** some bonds and FDs can't be sold or broken without a penalty.
5. **Reinvestment risk:** when your FD matures, rates may be lower than before.

### Role in a portfolio
Debt is the **stabiliser**. It provides predictable returns, cushions equity crashes, funds short-term goals and holds the emergency fund. It's not a wealth-builder in real terms.

## 4. Gold

### What it is
A physical commodity that has been a **store of value** for thousands of years. Indian households own an enormous amount of it (estimates run into tens of thousands of tonnes), mostly as jewellery.

### Where returns come from
Gold produces **no cash flow**: no earnings, no interest, no rent. Its return comes only from someone paying a higher price later. In India, the gold price depends on:
1. **The international gold price (in US dollars)**, driven by global fear, central bank buying, US interest rates and the dollar's strength.
2. **The USD/INR exchange rate.** When the rupee weakens, Indian gold prices rise even if global prices are flat. This has added roughly 3–4% a year to Indian gold returns over long periods.
3. Import duties and taxes.

### Behavior
- Over long periods, gold in rupees has returned roughly **inflation + a little**.
- It's **very lumpy**: long flat or falling stretches (e.g. 2012–2018) followed by sharp rises (2019–2020, 2024–2025).
- It often **rises during crises** (2008, 2020), when equity falls. This **low or negative correlation** with equity is what makes it a useful **diversifier**.

### How to own it (best to worst for investing)
1. **Gold ETFs:** traded on stock exchanges. Each unit is backed by physical gold of 99.5% purity. Low cost. Needs a demat account.
2. **Gold mutual funds (fund of funds):** invest in gold ETFs. No demat needed and SIPs are possible. Slightly higher cost.
3. **Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs):** issued by the RBI. Pay 2.5% a year interest *on top of* the gold price, and are tax-free at maturity. **The government stopped issuing new tranches in 2024**, so you can only buy existing ones on the exchange.
4. **Physical gold (coins, bars, jewellery):** making charges (8–25% on jewellery), purity concerns, storage and theft risk, and a loss on resale. Fine for cultural use, poor as an investment.
5. **Digital gold on apps:** **not regulated by SEBI or RBI**. Avoid.

### Role
A **5–15% allocation** as insurance against crises and rupee depreciation. It isn't a core growth asset.

## 5. Real estate

### What it is
Land and buildings: residential flats, commercial offices, shops, plots.

### Where returns come from
1. **Rental yield:** rent as a % of property value. Indian residential rental yields are low, typically **2–3%** (a ₹1 Cr flat might rent for ₹20–25k a month). Commercial property yields more (6–9%) but needs much more capital.
2. **Capital appreciation:** price growth. It varies hugely by city and decade. Many Indian cities saw flat or falling real prices between roughly 2013 and 2020.

### Drawbacks as an investment
- **Illiquid:** selling takes months.
- **Lumpy and indivisible:** you can't buy 5% of a flat or sell 10% when you need money.
- **High transaction costs:** stamp duty (5–7%), registration, brokerage and legal fees, often **7–10% of the value** in total.
- **Concentrated:** one property in one location.
- **Hidden costs:** maintenance, property tax, vacancy, tenant problems.
- **Leverage risk:** usually bought with a large home loan.
- **Legal risks:** title disputes, builder delays (RERA has helped, but the risk remains).

### Your own home ≠ investment
A house you live in is mostly **consumption**. It gives you shelter and security, which are valuable, but it doesn't produce cash flow for you, and you can't sell it without needing another place to live. Buy a home for life reasons, not as your "investment portfolio".

### Easier way in: REITs and InvITs
- **REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts):** own portfolios of rent-generating commercial property (offices, malls). Listed on exchanges, they must distribute most of their income, and they can be bought in small amounts. Examples: Embassy Office Parks, Mindspace, Brookfield, Nexus Select.
- **InvITs:** a similar structure for infrastructure (roads, power lines).

## 6. Cash and cash equivalents
- Savings account, liquid funds, overnight funds, sweep-in FDs.
- **Return:** low (3–7%), roughly equal to or below inflation.
- **Role:** emergency fund, transactions, short-term parking. It's the "oxygen" of personal finance. Without it, you're forced to sell other assets at bad times.

---

## 7. What "risk" actually means

Most people think risk = "losing money". In finance it's more precise:

1. **Volatility:** how much and how fast an investment's value swings. Measured by **standard deviation**. High volatility means a wide range of possible outcomes over short periods.
2. **Permanent loss of capital:** money that never comes back (the company goes bankrupt, a bond defaults, a fraud). This is the risk that really matters.
3. **Shortfall risk:** not having enough money when you need it, either because you invested too conservatively (inflation won) or too aggressively for a near-term goal (a crash hit at the wrong time).
4. **Liquidity risk:** you can't sell when you need cash.
5. **Behavioral risk:** *you* turn a temporary fall into a permanent loss by panic-selling.

**Key insight:** for a long-term investor in a diversified equity index, volatility is **temporary** if you don't sell. The two real risks are being **forced** to sell (no emergency fund, the goal comes due during a crash) and **choosing** to sell (panic). Both are solvable with planning.

## 8. The risk–return tradeoff

**Higher expected return only comes with higher risk.** Markets price risk: investors demand extra return (a "risk premium") for holding something more volatile or uncertain.

```
 Expected
 return ▲
        │                                        ● Small-cap equity
        │                                  ● Mid-cap equity
        │                           ● Large-cap equity
        │                  ● Real estate / Gold
        │           ● Corporate bonds
        │      ● Govt bonds / PPF / FD
        │   ● Liquid fund
        │ ● Savings account
        └────────────────────────────────────────────► Risk (volatility)
```

### Corollaries
- **There's no such thing as high return with low risk.** Any product promising "guaranteed 2% a month" or "double in 2 years" is either a fraud (Ponzi scheme) or hiding a risk you can't see.
- **The "equity risk premium":** equity's extra expected return over government bonds, historically about 4–6% a year in India. It's the reward for tolerating volatility.
- **Risk isn't always rewarded.** Concentrating in one stock adds risk without adding expected return. Only *diversified* market risk earns a premium.

## 9. Correlation: why mixing assets helps

**Correlation** measures how two assets move relative to each other:
- **+1:** always move together
- **0:** unrelated
- **−1:** always move in opposite directions

Equity and gold have historically had **low or negative correlation** in India, and equity and high-quality government bonds also tend to diverge during crises. When you combine assets that don't move together, **the portfolio's swings are smaller than the swings of its parts**, often without giving up much return. This is the foundation of **asset allocation** (Chapter 3.2).

## 10. Matching assets to time horizon

The single most useful rule: **choose the asset by when you need the money.**

| Need the money in… | Suitable assets | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Anytime (emergency) | Savings + liquid fund | Instant access, no volatility |
| < 1 year | Liquid fund, FD, arbitrage fund | Capital safety |
| 1–3 years | FDs, short-duration / corporate bond / banking-PSU debt funds | Limited rate sensitivity |
| 3–5 years | Mostly debt with some equity (conservative or balanced hybrid) | Some growth, limited downside |
| 5–7 years | Balanced: ~50–60% equity | Growth with a cushion |
| 7+ years | Mostly equity (70–90%), some debt and gold | Time absorbs volatility |

2. A debt fund has a modified duration of 4. Roughly how much will its NAV change if rates fall by 0.5%?
3. Why are Indian gold returns usually higher than global gold returns in dollars?
4. Give three reasons real estate is a difficult investment asset.
5. Why is equity "risky" for a 1-year goal but appropriate for a 15-year goal?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. New bonds pay the higher rate, so older, lower-coupon bonds must fall in price to offer a competitive yield.
2. About +2% (−4 × −0.5%).
3. The rupee has depreciated against the dollar over time, which adds to rupee gold prices.
4. Any three of: illiquid, high transaction costs, indivisible, concentrated, low rental yield, legal risk, leverage.
5. Volatility dominates over short periods (large losses are possible), while over long periods earnings growth dominates and negative returns become rare.
