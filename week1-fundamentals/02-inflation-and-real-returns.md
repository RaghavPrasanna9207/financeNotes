# Chapter 1.2: Inflation and Real Returns

> **Core**

> **What you'll learn:** what inflation is and why it happens, how India measures it (CPI, WPI) and the RBI's role, why your *personal* inflation is probably higher than the headline number, the difference between nominal and real returns, how tax makes "safe" investments lose money, and how to plan goals in future rupees.

---

## 1. What is inflation?

**Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises over time.** Put the other way round, it's the rate at which **the purchasing power of each rupee falls**.

If inflation is 6%, something that costs ₹100 today costs ₹106 next year. Your ₹100 note hasn't changed, but it now buys about 6% less.

### Why does inflation happen? (simple version)
1. **Demand-pull:** too much money chasing too few goods. When incomes, credit or government spending rise faster than production, prices rise.
2. **Cost-push:** production gets more expensive (crude oil, wages, raw materials), and companies pass the cost on.
3. **Built-in / expectations:** if everyone expects prices to rise, workers demand higher wages and companies raise prices in advance, so the expectation fulfils itself.
4. **Supply shocks:** in India, food inflation is very sensitive to monsoons, crop failures and supply disruptions (onions and tomatoes are the famous examples).
5. **Currency depreciation:** India imports most of its crude oil, along with electronics and gold. A weaker rupee makes imports costlier, which feeds into prices.

A small, stable amount of inflation (2–4%) is considered healthy for a growing economy. **Deflation** (falling prices) can be dangerous: people delay purchases, businesses cut back, and the economy can spiral down. **Hyperinflation** (Zimbabwe, Venezuela) destroys savings completely.

## 2. How India measures inflation

### CPI: Consumer Price Index (the one that matters to you)
- Published monthly by **MoSPI** (the National Statistics Office).
- Tracks the prices of a **basket of goods and services** a typical household buys: food, housing, clothing, fuel, transport, health, education and more.
- **Food has a large weight** in India's CPI basket (historically close to half), which makes headline inflation volatile when vegetable or pulse prices spike.
- **Headline CPI** = all items. **Core inflation** = CPI excluding food and fuel, a better measure of underlying, persistent inflation.

### WPI: Wholesale Price Index
- Tracks prices at the wholesale or producer level (manufactured goods, fuel, primary articles).
- Doesn't include services. Less relevant to household budgets, but it signals cost pressures that may reach consumers later.

### The RBI and inflation targeting
- Since 2016, India has had a formal **inflation-targeting framework**. The RBI's **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** aims to keep **CPI inflation at 4%, within a band of 2–6%**.
- The main tool is the **repo rate**, the rate at which the RBI lends to banks.
  - Inflation too high → RBI **raises** the repo rate → loans get costlier, borrowing and spending slow down, inflation cools.
  - Growth weak and inflation low → RBI **cuts** rates → loans get cheaper, spending rises.
- That's why FD rates, home-loan EMIs and bond prices all move when the RBI changes the repo rate. (You'll see why bond prices react in Chapter 1.3.)

### Long-run Indian inflation
Over the last few decades, Indian CPI inflation has averaged roughly **5–7% a year**, with spikes into double digits around 2009–2013 and calmer periods of 2–5%. For long-term planning, assuming **6%** for general expenses is a reasonable, slightly conservative default.

## 3. Your personal inflation is different

CPI is an *average* basket. Your actual basket may inflate much faster:

| Expense | Typical long-run inflation in India |
|---|---|
| General household (CPI) | ~5–6% |
| **Education** (school fees, college, coaching) | ~8–12% |
| **Healthcare** (hospitalisation, medicines) | ~10–14% |
| Housing / rent in big cities | ~5–8% |
| Lifestyle (eating out, travel, gadgets) | Varies, often 7%+ |

Also, as you earn more, you **upgrade** your lifestyle (lifestyle inflation): better phone, bigger flat, more travel. That's not technically inflation, but it has the same effect on how much money you need.

**Example:** an engineering or MBA degree that costs ₹20 lakh today, inflating at 10% a year, will cost about **₹83.5 lakh in 15 years**. Plan for *that* number, not ₹20 lakh.

## 4. Inflation's silent erosion

What ₹1,00,000 can buy in the future, at 6% inflation:

| After | Purchasing power of ₹1 L (in today's rupees) |
|---|---|
| 5 years | ₹74,700 |
| 10 years | ₹55,800 |
| 20 years | ₹31,200 |
| 30 years | ₹17,400 |

**Cash kept idle for 10 years loses about 44% of its purchasing power.** Nobody takes the money from you. It just quietly buys less.

And if your monthly expenses are ₹1 lakh today, at 6% inflation you'll need about **₹5.74 lakh a month** in 30 years just to live the same life. That's why retirement planning numbers look so large.

## 5. Nominal return vs real return

- **Nominal return:** the headline percentage your investment grows by. "This FD pays 7%."
- **Real return:** the growth in your **purchasing power**, i.e. what's left after inflation.

### The formulas
**Quick approximation:**
```
Real return ≈ Nominal return − Inflation
```
**Exact (Fisher equation):**
```
Real return = (1 + Nominal) / (1 + Inflation) − 1
```

**Example:** FD at 7%, inflation 6%.
- Approximate: 7 − 6 = 1%
- Exact: 1.07 / 1.06 − 1 = **0.94%**

The approximation is fine when the numbers are small. Use the exact formula when inflation or returns are high.

### Real return is what actually matters
If your investment grows 7% but prices grow 6%, you're only **0.94% richer** in terms of what you can buy. The other 6% of "growth" just kept you in place.

## 6. Adding tax: post-tax real return

Interest from FDs, savings accounts, RDs and (newer) debt funds is **taxed at your income-tax slab**, and for FDs that happens every year, even if you don't withdraw.

### The full calculation
```
Post-tax return = Nominal × (1 − tax rate)
Post-tax real return = (1 + post-tax return) / (1 + inflation) − 1
```

**FD at 7%, inflation 6%:**

| Tax slab | Post-tax return | Post-tax **real** return |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | 7.0% | +0.9% |
| 10% | 6.3% | +0.3% |
| 20% | 5.6% | **−0.4%** |
| 30% | 4.9% | **−1.0%** |

**Example:** ₹10 lakh in a 7% FD for 10 years, 30% tax bracket.
- Post-tax value: about **₹16.1 lakh**. It *looks* like you gained ₹6 lakh.
- In today's rupees: about **₹9.0 lakh**. **You've actually lost about 10% of your purchasing power.**

That's the core insight of this chapter: **"safe" investments are often guaranteed to lose purchasing power slowly**, once you account for inflation and tax. They're still useful for emergency funds and short-term goals, where safety and liquidity matter more than growth. But they can't build long-term wealth on their own.

## 7. Typical long-run returns in India (rough guide, not a promise)

| Asset | Nominal return (long run) | Approx. real return (at ~6% inflation) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash at home | 0% | −6% | Guaranteed loss |
| Savings account | 2.5–3.5% | −3% | For transactions only |
| Fixed deposit | 6–7.5% | 0 to +1.5% pre-tax | Negative post-tax at higher slabs |
| PPF | ~7–8% (tax-free) | +1 to +2% | Government-backed, 15-year lock-in |
| EPF | ~8–8.5% (mostly tax-free) | +2% | For salaried |
| Gold (in ₹) | ~9–11% over decades | +3–5% | Very lumpy: flat for years, then sharp jumps |
| Real estate (residential) | Highly variable, ~5–9% | 0–3% | Plus 2–3% rental yield. Illiquid. |
| Large-cap equity (Nifty 50 TRI) | ~11–13% | +5–7% | Volatile year to year |
| Mid/small-cap equity | ~12–15% | +6–9% | Much more volatile |

The only asset class that has *reliably* delivered strong real returns over long periods is **equity**. That's the core reason long-term goals should be equity-heavy, despite the volatility (Chapter 1.3).

## 8. Planning in future rupees: a worked example

**Goal:** a child's higher education in 15 years. Cost today: ₹20 lakh. Education inflation: 10%.

1. **Future cost** = 20,00,000 × (1.10)^15 = **₹83.5 lakh**
2. **Monthly SIP needed** at 12% expected return: `=PMT(12%/12, 180, 0, -8354496, 1)` ≈ **₹16,500/month**
3. If you'd assumed no inflation (₹20 L target), you'd have saved only about ₹4,000/month and been **₹60+ lakh short**.

**Rule:** for any goal more than 2–3 years away, always inflate the cost first, then calculate the investment needed.

---

## 9. What inflation means for your strategy

1. **Don't hold large amounts in savings accounts.** Keep only 1–2 months of expenses there.
2. **Emergency fund:** liquid funds or sweep FDs. Safety matters more than beating inflation here.
3. **Short-term goals (< 3 years):** FDs or debt funds. Accept a near-zero real return in exchange for certainty.
4. **Long-term goals (> 7 years):** equity is essential to beat inflation meaningfully.
5. **Plan with inflated numbers.** Use 6% for general expenses, 8–10% for education, 10%+ for healthcare.
6. **Increase your SIP every year** (step-up), at least in line with inflation, so your investing keeps its real value.

---

## 10. Summary

- Inflation = falling purchasing power. India targets 4% CPI (2–6% band). The RBI uses the repo rate to control it.
- Your personal inflation, especially for education and healthcare, is often well above CPI.
- **Real return = (1 + nominal)/(1 + inflation) − 1.** It measures how much richer you actually get.
- After inflation and tax, FDs and savings accounts often give **zero or negative** real returns.
- Plan every long-term goal in **future rupees**.
