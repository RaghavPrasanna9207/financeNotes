# Chapter 2.4: Index Funds vs Active Funds

> **Core**

> **What you'll learn:** what active and passive investing are, what the SPIVA India scorecards show, *why* most active funds lose to their index (cost, arithmetic, efficiency, survivorship), the problem of picking winners in advance, tracking error, when active funds might still make sense, and a practical core–satellite approach.

---

## 1. Two philosophies

### Active investing
- A **fund manager** and research team pick stocks they believe will **beat a benchmark** (e.g. Nifty 100 TRI for a large-cap fund).
- They decide what to buy, how much and when to sell.
- **Goal:** outperformance ("alpha").
- **Cost:** higher expense ratio (direct plans ~0.5–1.2%, regular plans ~1.5–2.1% plus statutory levies), plus higher trading costs from portfolio turnover.

### Passive investing
- The fund simply **copies an index**, holding the same stocks in the same weights.
- No stock picking or market predictions.
- **Goal:** match the index return, minus tiny costs.
- **Cost:** very low (Nifty 50 index funds' direct plans are often 0.05–0.2%).

Jack Bogle, founder of Vanguard and creator of the first retail index fund (1976), argued: *"Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack."*

---

## 2. What the data says: SPIVA India

**SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active)** is a scorecard published twice a year by **S&P Dow Jones Indices**. It measures what percentage of active funds in each category **underperformed** their benchmark over 1, 3, 5 and 10 years. It corrects for **survivorship bias** by including funds that closed or merged.

### The latest numbers: SPIVA India Mid-Year 2026 (periods ending 30 June 2026)
**% of active funds that underperformed their benchmark** (funds that closed or merged count as failures):

| Category | Benchmark | 3 yrs | 5 yrs | 10 yrs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large-cap | S&P India LargeMidCap | 80% | 85% | 74% |
| ELSS | S&P India BMI | 64% | 71% | 80% |
| Mid/small-cap | S&P India SmallCap | 53% | 55% | 82% |
| Composite bond | iBoxx ALBI India | 75% | 76% | 93% |
| Government bond | iBoxx ALBI India | 80% | 91% | 75% |

**Survivorship:** only **74%** of funds across all categories survived the full 10 years. About a quarter were closed or merged, usually after performing badly.

Source: [SPIVA India Scorecard, Mid-Year 2026](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-india-scorecard-mid-year-2026.pdf). S&P changed the benchmarks in mid-2024, so older reports that use the S&P BSE 100 for large caps aren't directly comparable.

### What the numbers say
1. **Large-cap:** about three in four active funds lagged over 10 years, and 80–85% over 3 and 5 years.
2. **Mid/small-cap:** active funds did *relatively* better over 3 and 5 years, where only about half lagged. **Over 10 years they did worse than large caps (82% lagged)**, so the edge didn't last.
3. **ELSS:** the longer the period, the worse it looks: 64% lagged at 3 years and 80% at 10 years.
4. **Bonds:** active debt funds fared poorly too, with 93% of composite bond funds lagging over 10 years.
5. **No category** had a majority of active funds beating the index over 10 years.

> The exact percentages change with every edition (published twice a year). Check the latest report before quoting numbers.

### Persistence: do winners keep winning?
S&P also publishes **persistence scorecards**. They track whether top-quartile funds stay in the top quartile in later periods. The answer is consistently **rarely**: only a small fraction of top-performing funds remain top performers a few years later, often no better than chance.

**Implication:** picking last year's (or last 5 years') top fund is not a reliable way to pick next decade's top fund.

## 3. Why most active funds underperform

### Reason 1: Costs (the biggest one)
An active large-cap fund charging 1% more than an index fund has to **beat the market by 1% every year just to break even**. Over 15–20 years, that hurdle compounds.

Add **trading costs** (brokerage, STT, market impact) from portfolio turnover, which often run 30–100% a year in active funds, and the hurdle is higher still.

### Reason 2: The arithmetic of active management
This idea comes from Nobel laureate William Sharpe:
1. All investors together hold the **entire market**.
2. Before costs, the **average investor earns the market return**. (Some beat it, some lag, and the gains and losses cancel.)
3. Passive investors earn exactly the market return (minus tiny costs).
4. So **active investors as a group must also earn the market return before costs**.
5. After their higher costs, **the average active rupee must underperform the average passive rupee**.

This isn't an opinion or a forecast. It's arithmetic.

### Reason 3: Market efficiency (especially in large caps)
- India's top 100 stocks are followed by dozens of analysts, domestic and foreign institutions, and algorithmic traders.
- New information is priced in within minutes. Finding a lasting **edge** is very hard.
- Mid and small caps are less researched, so there's more room for skill. That shows up over 3 to 5 years, when only about half of mid/small-cap active funds lag. Over 10 years, though, 82% lagged, worse than large caps (SPIVA Mid-Year 2026).

### Reason 4: Structural constraints (SEBI rules + size)
- Large-cap funds must hold at least 80% in the top 100. Their universe is small and they can't deviate much.
- Large funds (tens of thousands of crores) can't buy meaningful positions in smaller companies without moving prices.
- Many active funds end up as **closet indexers**: portfolios close to the index but charging active fees.

### Reason 5: Behavioral and career incentives
- Managers fear underperforming peers more than they want to beat the index, so they herd.
- Fund houses launch new funds in hot themes, and assets flow to recent winners just before they revert.

## 4. "Just pick the good active fund"

This is the obvious response, and the hardest thing to do.
- **Past performance is a weak predictor** of future outperformance (persistence data).
- Star managers leave, retire or change style. The AMC's strategy changes. Assets swell and outperformance fades.
- You need to identify a winner **in advance**, hold it through inevitable bad years, and know when to leave. All without hindsight.
- Ratings (stars) are largely based on past returns.

With an index fund, there's no "picking" problem. You get the market return, reliably, minus a tiny fee.

---

## 5. Advantages and disadvantages of index funds

### Advantages
1. **Low cost.** The single biggest predictor of future fund returns is cost.
2. **Guaranteed to get (close to) the market return.** You'll never badly underperform the market.
3. **No manager risk.** No star manager leaving, no style drift, no bad bets.
4. **Transparent.** You always know exactly what you own.
5. **Self-cleansing.** Index rebalancing replaces declining companies with rising ones (Chapter 1.5).
6. **Low turnover.** Fewer trading costs inside the fund.
7. **Simple.** Easy to understand, compare and stick with. That improves *behavior*, which matters most.

### Disadvantages and limitations
1. **You'll never beat the market.** You get the market minus a bit.
2. **You own everything,** including overvalued stocks. Market-cap weighting means the biggest (sometimes most expensive) stocks get the largest weights.
3. **Concentration:** the Nifty 50's top 10 stocks often make up more than half of the index, and financial services are a large slice.
4. **No downside protection.** An index fund falls with the market. No manager will move to cash.
5. **Tracking error/difference.** Small deviations from the index (next section).

---

## 6. Tracking error and tracking difference

An index fund can't match its index perfectly. Why?
- **Expense ratio:** deducted daily.
- **Cash drag:** a small amount is kept as cash for redemptions.
- **Transaction costs:** when money flows in or out, or when the index rebalances.
- **Dividend timing:** dividends are received with a delay.
- **Impact cost:** buying or selling large quantities can move prices.

Two measures:

| Measure | Definition | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Tracking difference** | Index return − fund return over a period (e.g. 1 year) | **How much less you actually earned** than the index. Roughly the expense ratio plus other leakage. |
| **Tracking error** | Standard deviation of the daily (or periodic) return differences, annualised | **How consistently** the fund follows the index. Low = tight tracking. |

- SEBI requires AMCs to disclose tracking error for index funds and ETFs, with limits (e.g. tracking error generally should not exceed 2%).
- Good Nifty 50 index funds have **tracking error well under 0.1–0.2%** and **tracking difference close to their expense ratio**.
- When choosing between index funds tracking the same index, these two numbers plus the expense ratio are what matter (Chapter 2.8).

## 7. When might an active fund make sense?

Active management isn't always pointless:
- **Mid and small caps:** less efficient segments, where active funds have had better odds over 3 to 5 years. Over 10 years, though, most still lagged (82% in SPIVA Mid-Year 2026), so low-cost index funds (Midcap 150, Smallcap 250) are a strong alternative.
- **Flexi cap:** a manager with true freedom to go anywhere, if you want one active fund.
- **Debt:** there aren't many passive options for some needs (though target maturity funds and gilt index funds now exist).
- **Specific strategies** you understand and believe in (e.g. value investing with a disciplined, low-turnover manager).

**If you choose active, look for:**
- A long, consistent record versus the **TRI benchmark**, through *rolling returns* rather than point-to-point returns
- The same manager or team for most of that period
- A reasonable expense ratio (direct plan), and moderate fund size relative to the category
- Low portfolio turnover and a clear, stable philosophy
- **A real difference from the index** (high "active share"), not a closet indexer

## 8. The practical approach: core and satellite

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│   CORE (60–100% of equity)                  │
│   Low-cost index funds                      │
│   e.g. Nifty 50, + Nifty Next 50 / Midcap   │
│   150, or one Nifty 500 / total-market fund │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│   SATELLITE (0–40%, optional)               │
│   1–2 active funds (flexi cap / mid cap)    │
│   or international index FoF                │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

- The **core** guarantees market returns at low cost.
- The **satellite** lets you try for extra returns without betting everything on one manager.
- **Totally fine:** 100% core, i.e. just one or two index funds for life.

---

## 9. Summary

- **Active** funds try to beat the index. **Passive** (index) funds copy it at very low cost.
- **SPIVA India (Mid-Year 2026):** over 10 years, 74% of active large-cap funds, 80% of ELSS and 82% of mid/small-cap funds underperformed, and only 74% of funds survived. Winners rarely persist.
- **Why:** costs, the arithmetic of active management, efficient large-cap markets, SEBI constraints, and size.
- Index funds aren't perfect: tracking difference, concentration, no downside protection. But they reliably deliver the market return.
- Use **index funds as the core**. Use active funds, if at all, as a small satellite, especially in mid caps or flexi caps.
