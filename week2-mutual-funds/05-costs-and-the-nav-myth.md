# Chapter 2.5: Costs: Expense Ratio, Direct vs Regular, Exit Load, and the NAV Myth

> **What you'll learn:** what the expense ratio includes and how it's deducted, SEBI's TER limits, how much fees cost you over a lifetime (with numbers), the direct vs regular plan difference and the distributor commission system, exit loads, other hidden costs, and why a "low NAV" fund is not "cheap".

---

## 1. The expense ratio (TER: Total Expense Ratio)

### What it is
The **annual fee** a mutual fund charges to run the scheme, expressed as a **percentage of your investment** (of the fund's assets).

### What it covers
- **Fund management fee** (the AMC's income)
- Administrative costs: RTA, custodian, audit, legal, registrar
- Marketing and selling expenses
- **Distributor commission** (only in regular plans)
- GST on management fees

Brokerage and transaction costs for buying and selling securities are charged on top, within limits, which is another reason **portfolio turnover** matters.

### How it's charged: invisibly
- **You never get a bill.** The expense is deducted **daily** from the fund's assets, reducing the NAV. A 1% TER means about 1/365th of 1% is shaved off the NAV every day.
- **All published returns are already net of expenses.** That's why direct plans show higher returns than regular plans of the same fund.

### SEBI limits on TER
SEBI caps the TER on a sliding scale: the larger the fund, the lower the maximum percentage. Roughly:

| Fund assets (AUM) | Max TER, equity schemes | Max TER, debt schemes |
|---|---|---|
| First ₹500 crore | 2.25% | 2.00% |
| Next ₹250 crore | 2.00% | 1.75% |
| Next ₹1,250 crore | 1.75% | 1.50% |
| … continues to fall in slabs … | | |
| Above ₹50,000 crore | ~1.05% | ~0.80% |

- **Index funds and ETFs:** max TER of **1.00%**, but good ones charge far less (0.05–0.30% for direct Nifty 50 index funds).
- **Fund of Funds:** separate caps, depending on what they invest in.
- SEBI periodically reviews these caps. The trend has been toward lower costs.

AMCs must disclose TER changes on their websites and notify investors before increasing it.

### Typical expense ratios (direct plans) in practice
| Fund type | Typical direct TER |
|---|---|
| Nifty 50 / Sensex index fund | 0.05–0.25% |
| Nifty 50 ETF | 0.02–0.10% |
| Midcap / smallcap index fund | 0.20–0.40% |
| Active large-cap / flexi-cap | 0.5–1.0% |
| Active mid/small cap | 0.4–1.0% |
| Liquid fund | 0.1–0.3% |
| Regular plans | Add roughly **0.5–1.5%** to the above |

## 2. Why small percentages are big money

A 1% difference sounds trivial. Over long periods, it compounds.

### Example 1: SIP of ₹10,000/month for 20 years (₹24 L invested)
| Net return (after fees) | Final value | Lost to fees (vs 12%) |
|---|---|---|
| 12.0% (gross 12.2%, TER 0.2%) | **₹99.9 L** | — |
| 11.0% (TER ~1.2%) | ₹87.4 L | **₹12.5 L** |
| 10.5% (TER ~1.7%) | ₹81.8 L | **₹18.1 L** |

### Example 2: lump sum of ₹10 L for 20 years
| Net return | Final value |
|---|---|
| 12% | ₹96.5 L |
| 11% | ₹80.6 L |
| **Difference** | **₹15.9 L**, from just a 1% annual fee difference |

### Example 3: 25-year SIP of ₹10,000/month
| TER | Net return | Corpus |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2% | 11.8% | ~₹1.83 Cr |
| 1.2% | 10.8% | ~₹1.54 Cr |
| 2.0% | 10.0% | ~₹1.34 Cr |

A 1.8% higher fee costs about **₹50 lakh** over 25 years, which is more than double the ₹30 L you actually invested.

### The key insight
You **can't control** future market returns. You **can control** costs. And costs are certain: you pay them every year, whether the fund does well or badly. **Research consistently finds that low cost is one of the most reliable predictors of better long-term fund outcomes.**

## 3. Direct plans vs regular plans

Since January 2013, every mutual fund scheme must offer two plans:

| | **Direct plan** | **Regular plan** |
|---|---|---|
| How you buy | Directly from the AMC or a direct-plan platform | Through a **distributor** (bank RM, agent, broker, some apps) |
| Distributor commission | **None** | **Yes**: paid every year, as long as you stay invested ("trail commission") |
| Expense ratio | Lower | Higher, typically **0.5–1.5% more** |
| Portfolio | **Identical** | Identical |
| Fund manager | Same | Same |
| NAV | **Higher** (it compounds faster) | Lower |
| Returns | Higher by exactly the TER difference | Lower |

### How to tell which plan you're in
- The scheme name says **"Direct"** or **"Regular"**: e.g. "XYZ Flexi Cap Fund – **Direct** Plan – Growth".
- Your CAS shows the plan and, for regular plans, the **ARN (AMFI Registration Number)** of the distributor earning commission.
- Banks and "free" financial planners selling you mutual funds are almost always selling **regular plans**.

### Why regular plans still exist
- Distributors provide service: paperwork, reminders, hand-holding. Some investors value that.
- But the commission creates a **conflict of interest**: distributors may push funds with higher commissions, NFOs or frequent switching.

### The better alternative if you want advice
- A **SEBI-Registered Investment Adviser (RIA)** charges you a **flat or percentage fee directly** and recommends **direct plans**. Their income doesn't depend on which products you buy.
- Verify registration on SEBI's website ("Intermediaries/Market Infrastructure Institutions → Investment Advisers").

### Switching from regular to direct
- Switching is treated as a **redemption + fresh purchase**.
- It can trigger:
  - **Capital gains tax** (on the units sold)
  - **Exit load** (if within the load period)
- **Smart approach:**
  1. Start all **new SIPs** in direct plans immediately.
  2. For existing regular units, switch those **already past the exit-load period** and with **long-term** status, ideally using the ₹1.25 L annual tax-free LTCG limit on equity.
  3. Spread switches over a few financial years if gains are large.
- For ELSS, units can only be switched after their 3-year lock-in.

## 4. Exit load

### What it is
A fee charged when you **redeem units before a specified period**, as a % of the **redemption value**. It discourages short-term trading in long-term funds, and it goes back into the scheme (benefiting the remaining investors).

### Typical exit loads
| Fund type | Typical exit load |
|---|---|
| Equity funds (active) | **1% if redeemed within 12 months** (some 365 days, some 90 days), nil after |
| Index funds | Often nil, or small (0.1–0.25%) within a few days or weeks |
| ETFs | None (you sell on the exchange) |
| ELSS | None, but there's a 3-year lock-in |
| Liquid funds | Graded load for **days 1–6** (0.0070% down to 0.0045%), nil from day 7 |
| Debt funds | Varies: nil to 0.25–1% within a few months |
| Hybrid funds | Often 1% within 1 year (sometimes on units above a free limit, e.g. 10% free) |

### Example
You invested ₹2,00,000 in an equity fund. Eight months later it's worth ₹2,20,000 and you redeem. **Exit load (1%) = ₹2,200.** You receive ₹2,17,800 (before tax).

### Applied per instalment (FIFO)
For SIPs, each instalment has its own clock. Units are redeemed **first-in, first-out**. If you redeem 13 months after starting a SIP, the first instalment is load-free but later instalments may still attract the load.

**Always check the exit load** in the scheme information document, factsheet or fund page before investing, especially for money you might need within a year.

## 5. Other costs to know

| Cost | Details |
|---|---|
| **Stamp duty** | **0.005%** on every purchase (₹5 per ₹1 lakh). Tiny. |
| **Securities Transaction Tax (STT)** | 0.001% on redemption of equity funds (tiny). Higher for ETFs sold on exchanges. |
| **Taxes on gains** | The biggest "cost" if you trade frequently (Chapter 3.5) |
| **ETF costs** | Brokerage + STT + DP charges + **bid–ask spread** + premium/discount to NAV |
| **Portfolio turnover costs** | Hidden inside the fund. High turnover means higher costs. |
| **Cash drag** | Uninvested cash in a fund earns less in rising markets |
| **"Platform fees"** | Some apps charge subscriptions. Most direct platforms are free. |

## 6. The NAV myth: "Low NAV = cheap fund"

This is one of the most common beginner mistakes.

### The wrong intuition
"Fund A has a NAV of ₹15 and Fund B has a NAV of ₹450. Fund A is cheaper, so I'll get more units and more growth."

### Why it's wrong
NAV is **not a price that reflects value** like a share price relative to earnings. It's just:
```
NAV = Total portfolio value / Number of units
```
A fund's NAV depends on **when it was launched** (most start at ₹10) and **how much it has grown since**. A high NAV usually means an **older fund that has compounded for longer**.

### Proof with an example
You invest ₹10,000 in each of two funds with **identical portfolios**:

| | Fund A | Fund B |
|---|---|---|
| NAV | ₹10 | ₹100 |
| Units you get | 1,000 | 100 |
| Portfolio rises 10% → new NAV | ₹11 | ₹110 |
| **Your investment value** | 1,000 × 11 = **₹11,000** | 100 × 110 = **₹11,000** |

**Identical outcome.** What matters is the **percentage change in NAV**, which depends entirely on what the fund holds, not on the NAV level.

### Corollaries
- **NFOs at ₹10 are not bargains** (Chapter 2.1).
- **More units ≠ more money.** Units are just slices. Cutting a pizza into 8 slices instead of 4 doesn't give you more pizza.
- Comparing NAVs between two funds tells you nothing about which is better.

### The related IDCW myth
"This fund pays a ₹2 dividend per unit, so I'm earning extra." No. When the fund pays ₹2 per unit, the **NAV falls by ₹2**. Your total wealth is unchanged, except that you now owe **tax** on the payout at your slab rate. That's why the option is now called "Income Distribution cum **Capital Withdrawal**".

### What actually matters when comparing funds
- The **category** (what it invests in)
- **Expense ratio** (cost)
- **Track record vs benchmark** (consistency, rolling returns)
- **Risk metrics** (volatility, drawdowns)
- For index funds: **tracking difference and tracking error**

## 7. Summary

- The **expense ratio** is deducted daily from NAV. You never see a bill, but it compounds against you.
- A **1% annual fee difference** can cost **₹12–50 lakh** over 20–25 years on typical SIPs.
- **Always choose Direct plans.** Regular plans pay a trail commission (0.5–1.5% a year) for the same portfolio. If you want advice, pay a fee-only SEBI RIA.
- **Exit loads** (usually 1% within a year for equity funds) apply per instalment, FIFO.
- **NAV level is irrelevant.** A ₹10 NAV is not cheaper than a ₹500 NAV. IDCW payouts aren't extra money either.

4. Fund X has a NAV of ₹12, Fund Y ₹350, and both track the Nifty 50 with identical costs. Which gives better returns?
5. Why might switching all your regular-plan holdings to direct in one day be a bad idea?

4. Neither. Returns depend on the portfolio and costs, not the NAV level, so they'll be the same.
