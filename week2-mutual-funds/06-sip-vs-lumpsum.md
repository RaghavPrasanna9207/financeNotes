# Chapter 2.6: SIP vs Lump Sum (and STP, SWP)

> **Core**

> **What you'll learn:** how a SIP works mechanically, rupee cost averaging (and its limits), why the real power of SIPs is behavioral, step-up SIPs, when a lump sum is better, how to invest a windfall with an STP, SIP myths, what to do during crashes, and how SWP works for withdrawals.

---

## 1. What a SIP is

A **Systematic Investment Plan (SIP)** is a **method** of investing a fixed amount in a mutual fund at regular intervals (usually monthly), automatically debited from your bank account.

- **It's not a product.** It's just a way of buying a mutual fund. "I invest in SIP" really means "I invest in a mutual fund through SIP".
- Minimums are often ₹100–500 a month.
- You choose the **amount**, **date** and **frequency** (monthly is most common; daily, weekly and quarterly also exist).
- It's set up with an **auto-debit mandate** (e-NACH or UPI AutoPay).
- You can **pause, modify or stop** it any time, without penalty. Exit loads apply only when you redeem.
- **Each SIP instalment is a separate purchase**, with its own NAV, units, holding period (for tax), exit-load clock and lock-in (for ELSS).

---

## 2. Rupee cost averaging

### How it works
A fixed amount buys **more units when the NAV is low** and **fewer units when it's high**. Over time, your **average cost per unit** ends up **lower than the average NAV** over the period.

### Worked example: ₹1,000/month
| Month | Amount | NAV | Units bought |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 1,000 | 100 | 10.00 |
| Feb | 1,000 | 80 | 12.50 |
| Mar | 1,000 | 60 | 16.67 |
| Apr | 1,000 | 80 | 12.50 |
| May | 1,000 | 100 | 10.00 |
| **Total** | **5,000** | Avg NAV = **84** | **61.67 units** |

- Your **average cost per unit** = 5,000 / 61.67 = **₹81.08**, which is lower than the average NAV of ₹84.
- At the end, the NAV is back to ₹100, where it started. Your 61.67 units are worth **₹6,167**, a **23% gain**, even though the market ended exactly where it began.

**This is the key insight: for a SIP investor, falling markets are good while you're still accumulating.** The SIP bought 16.67 units at the bottom (₹60).

### Why it works mathematically
Average cost = the **harmonic mean** of the prices, which is always ≤ the **arithmetic mean**. That gap exists whenever prices fluctuate.

### The limits of rupee cost averaging
- It **doesn't guarantee profit**. If the market falls and *stays* down, you still lose money.
- In a steadily **rising** market, a SIP ends up buying at higher and higher prices. A lump sum at the start would have done better.
- Its benefit shrinks over time: once your corpus is large, each month's SIP is a small fraction of the total, so it barely changes your average cost.

---

## 3. The real power of SIP: behavior

The mathematical benefit is modest. The **behavioral benefits are enormous**:

1. **It matches how you earn.** Most people receive income monthly, so investing monthly is natural.
2. **Automation removes decisions.** You don't need to decide "is this a good time?" every month. Decisions are where people go wrong.
3. **It removes market timing.** Nobody consistently times markets. SIP makes timing irrelevant.
4. **It turns crashes into opportunities.** A SIP investor can actually *welcome* a crash, because the SIP buys cheap.
5. **"Pay yourself first."** If investing happens on salary day, before spending, you spend what's left instead of investing what's left.
6. **Discipline and habit.** Over years, consistency matters more than brilliance.

> Morgan Housel: the best financial plan is one you can stick with. SIPs are the easiest plan to stick with.

## 4. Step-up (top-up) SIP

A **step-up SIP** automatically increases your SIP amount every year, by a fixed amount (e.g. +₹1,000) or a percentage (e.g. +10%).

### Why it matters
- Your income should rise over time. If your SIP doesn't, your savings rate falls.
- Inflation erodes the real value of a fixed SIP.

### Impact: ₹5,000/month, 12% return, 20 years
| | Total invested | Final value |
|---|---|---|
| Flat SIP | ₹12.0 L | ~₹50 L |
| **10% annual step-up** | ~₹34.4 L | **~₹99 L** |

You can set this up on most platforms when starting the SIP, or just increase the SIP manually every year after your appraisal.

## 5. Lump sum investing

A **lump sum** is a one-time investment of a large amount: a bonus, inheritance, maturity proceeds, gifts.

### The math favours lump sum (usually)
- Markets go up more often than they go down, so on average, **money invested earlier has more time in the market**.
- Studies across many markets (including Vanguard's research on US, UK and Australian data) find that **investing a lump sum immediately beats spreading it out over 6–12 months roughly two-thirds of the time**.
- Indian data shows similar results over long periods.

### The psychology favours spreading it out
- If you invest ₹10 lakh today and the market falls 25% next month, you're down ₹2.5 lakh. Many people panic and sell, turning a temporary fall into a permanent loss.
- **Regret** is a powerful emotion. Spreading the investment reduces the worst-case regret.
- A slightly worse expected outcome you can **stick with** beats a better expected outcome you abandon.

## 6. Investing a lump sum: the STP

A **Systematic Transfer Plan (STP)** combines both approaches:
1. Put the lump sum into a **liquid fund or ultra-short fund** (earning ~6–7%, low risk).
2. Set up an automatic **transfer of a fixed amount every week or month** into the target equity fund (same AMC).
3. Over 6–12 months, the money moves into equity gradually.

**Example:** ₹6 lakh bonus. Invest it all in XYZ Liquid Fund, then STP ₹50,000/month into XYZ Nifty 50 Index Fund for 12 months.

### Guidelines
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Lump sum is small relative to your existing portfolio | Invest it at once |
| Large lump sum, long horizon, you won't panic in a crash | **Invest at once** (or over 2–3 months) |
| Large lump sum, you're nervous or new to equity | **STP over 6–12 months** |
| Lump sum meant for a goal < 3–5 years away | It shouldn't go into equity at all. Use debt. |

Don't stretch an STP beyond about 12 months. Longer periods mean more time out of the market with little extra benefit.

## 7. SIP myths

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| "SIP is a type of mutual fund / product." | It's a payment method for buying a mutual fund. |
| "SIPs are safe / guaranteed." | A SIP in an equity fund carries equity risk. SIP returns can be negative for 1–3 years. |
| "Stop the SIP when the market is falling." | **The worst thing you can do.** Falling markets are when SIPs buy the most units. |
| "Start SIPs only when the market corrects." | Waiting is market timing. Time in the market beats timing the market. |
| "The SIP date matters a lot (1st vs 15th vs 28th)." | Studies show the date makes negligible difference over long periods. Pick one right after salary day. |
| "More SIPs in more funds = more diversification." | Usually just overlap. 2–4 funds are enough. |
| "Daily SIPs are better than monthly." | Practically no difference over the long term, and more transactions to track. |
| "If my SIP is negative after 1 year, the fund is bad." | One year is too short for equity. Judge over 5+ years against the benchmark. |

---

## 8. What to do when markets crash

Every SIP investor will live through several 20–40% falls. A plan:

1. **Don't stop the SIP.** This is when it does its best work.
2. **Don't check your portfolio daily.** Volatility feels worse the more often you look.
3. **Check your emergency fund.** If it's intact, nothing forces you to sell.
4. **Check your goal timelines.** Is any goal within 2–3 years that should have been moved to debt already? (Glide path: Chapter 3.2.)
5. **Optional:** if you have extra cash and a long horizon, add a lump sum or increase the SIP.
6. **Rebalance** if your equity allocation has fallen far below target (Chapter 3.2). This means *buying* equity.

**History:** after the 2008 crash (Nifty about −60%), the 2020 COVID crash (about −38%) and others, the Indian market recovered and went on to new highs within a few years. SIP investors who continued through those periods saw some of their best returns from units bought at the bottom.

## 9. SWP: Systematic Withdrawal Plan

The reverse of a SIP: you **withdraw a fixed amount every month** from a fund.

### Uses
- Regular income in retirement
- Funding a known expense stream (e.g. college fees)

### Why SWP beats IDCW for income
- **Tax:** with IDCW, the full payout is taxed at your slab. With SWP, each withdrawal is part **principal** (not taxed) and part **gain** (taxed as capital gains, at lower rates, and for equity only above the ₹1.25 L LTCG exemption).
- **Control:** you decide the amount and timing. IDCW amounts are decided by the fund.

### Caution
Withdrawing too much from an equity fund during a prolonged crash can deplete the corpus fast ("sequence of returns risk"). Retirement SWPs usually come from a **debt or hybrid fund**, refilled periodically from equity.

---

## 10. Summary

- A **SIP** is a disciplined method, not a product. Each instalment is a separate purchase.
- **Rupee cost averaging** lowers your average cost in volatile markets, but doesn't guarantee profit.
- The **biggest benefit of SIP is behavioral**: automation, no timing, discipline.
- Use a **step-up SIP** to grow your investing with your income.
- **Lump sum** wins mathematically about two-thirds of the time. Use an **STP** over 6–12 months if you're nervous.
- **Never stop SIPs during crashes.**
- For withdrawals, use **SWP** rather than IDCW.
